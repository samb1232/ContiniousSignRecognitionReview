Abstract
Background:Sign languages are the primary communication mode for Deaf and Hard of Hearing (DHH) commu-
nities, yet automated recognition research has concentrated on American and British Sign Languages, leaving Indian
Sign Language (ISL) and its regional variants comparatively neglected despite an estimated 18 million users.
Methods:Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, a systematic search of multiple databases (Scopus, IEEE Xplore,
ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, and Google Scholar) was conducted in October 2025 for studies published
between January 2020 and October 2025. From 287 identified records, 47 studies were retained after title and
abstract screening (Cohen’sκ = 0.87) and full-text assessment against predefined inclusion criteria. Study quality
was appraised using an adapted QUADAS-2 framework.
Results:Reported accuracy across individual studies rose with architectural evolution: CNN-based models 79–
92%, CNN-LSTM hybrids 88–97%, pose-based methods 89–94%, and transformer-based approaches 91–98% under
controlled conditions. The distribution of research was highly uneven across the nine regional sign languages, with
Telugu Sign Language showing a roughly 16-fold per-capita deficit relative to Kannada. Persistent barriers included
continuous-recognition performance gaps, signer dependence (5–18% accuracy drops), environmental sensitivity
(8–15% drops), and severe data scarcity for regional variants.
Conclusions:The review proposes the ISLR-Bench standardization framework and a 2026–2030 research roadmap
spanning self-supervised learning, cross-lingual transfer, edge optimization, and community-centered design, alongside
accessibility targets for government digital platforms and DHH employment.

1. Introduction
Globally, sign languages represent the most used communication mode for DHH people; every language has its own
unique structural features that remain uninfluenced by the spoken language around them. Despite the fact that
automated sign language recognition systems are widely regarded as essential components of accessibility infrastructure,
the existing research has largely focused on ASL and BSL, while Indian sign language and its variants have been left
mostly unnoticed. According to the 2021 Census, Indian Sign Language is the main language for 5–7 million DHH
individuals [1], but the existence of different regional variants makes the total number of users who need access at least
18 million, drawn from India’s linguistically diverse population [2, 3].
The years 2020–2025 have been marked by the beginning of a new era of technology for ISL recognition, characterized
by rapid architectural evolution, dataset expansion, and the first cases of the technology being put into real-world use.
This increase in publications coincides with three noteworthy developments and the widespread use of transformer
architectures, with a growing proportion of recent studies employing transformer-based architectures, reflecting a shift
toward attention-driven models for sign language recognition in 2023 relative to 2022. Despite the progress made, there
remain a great number of inequalities in the research distribution across the different regional languages. The analysis
shows that Kannada Sign Language, with its 3.3 million speakers, has 6 published studies [4–7], while Telugu Sign
Language, with its 8.1 million speakers, has only 2 studies [8], the research per capita for the latter being 16 times
lower. Research efforts are mainly concentrated in one area, the Bangalore-Hyderabad corridor, and the Northeast
region remains under-served even though it has four regional language variants [2].
1.1. Sociolinguistic Context and Accessibility Barriers
The DHH community in India suffers from serious accessibility problems that have been outlined clearly before, and
therefore technological solutions are essential to address these challenges. The literacy rates of DHH individuals are
noted to be only 55%, while the general population’s literacy rate is 74% [1, 9]; the incidence of preventable diseases
is 40% higher due to lack of communication in healthcare [10]; and employment rates are 10–25% lower than those
of hearing people, with communication barriers identified as the main obstacle to employment [11]. The Indian Sign
Language Research and Training Centre (ISLRTC) reports a chronic shortage of certified sign language interpreters in
education, healthcare, and government sectors across India [12].
1.2. Research Questions and Scope
This paper investigates the following research questions. (RQ1) What has driven the development of deep learning
architectures from 2020 to 2025, and what have been the resulting changes in performance, efficiency, and generalization
through CNNs, CNN-LSTM hybrids, pose estimation, and transformers? (RQ2) How well has research on the nine
regional sign languages of India been conducted, where are the gaps in the research, and what are the performance
differences between the regional variants? (RQ3) What are the technical, computational, and sociolinguistic aspects
that hinder the performance of real-world systems, particularly the decline in performance with new signers (5–18%
accuracy drops), environmental changes (8–15% drops), and differences in regional dialects? The trends of publications
in sign language research over the period 2020–2025 are shown in Figure 1.
Studies published between January 1, 2020 and October 31, 2025 were included. A systematic search of the literature
was conducted across multiple databases in October 2025.
2. Methodology
This structured review follows PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The completed PRISMA 2020 checklist (Supplementary Table
S1) and full search strategies (Supplementary Appendix S2) are provided and summarized in Figure 2.
2.1. Search Strategy and Study Selection
Exact Boolean, database-specific search strategies were employed. A representative query used in Scopus was: (“Indian
Sign Language” OR “Kannada Sign Language” OR “Tamil Sign Language” OR “Gujarati Sign Language” OR “Marathi
Sign Language” OR “Telugu Sign Language” OR “Assamese Sign Language” OR “Bangla Sign Language” OR “Punjabi
Sign Language”) AND (“deep learning” OR “CNN” OR “LSTM” OR “transformer” OR “pose estimation”) AND
(“recognition” OR “translation”).

Equivalent syntax-adjusted queries were used for IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, and Google Scholar.
Full database-specific queries are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Initially, 287 records were retrieved from
the searches. When the EndNote program was used to eliminate duplicates, 152 distinct research articles remained
for the next step of title and abstract evaluation. The citation tracking process supported the database searches by
tracing back the references of the 15 most-cited papers and conducting forward tracking to identify recent studies citing
the foundational works. Screening was carried out by two independent reviewers with Cohen’sκ = 0.87inter-rater
agreement on 152 records. Title and abstract screening identified 89 papers that might be relevant, among which 18
borderline cases were settled by consensus discussion. The full-text review of the 89 papers against strict inclusion
criteria resulted in a final corpus of 47 papers. Of the papers reviewed, 42 were excluded with documented reasons: 28
papers dealt with non-Indian sign languages, 8 lacked sufficient methodological detail, and 8 presented hardware-only
systems without machine learning components.
2.2. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
The inclusion requirements specified that studies should span January 2020 to October 2025. The studies had to
address Indian Sign Language or documented regional variants, apply deep learning techniques (CNNs, RNNs/LSTMs,
transformers, or hybrid architectures), and present performance metrics quantitatively (accuracy, F1-score, Word Error
Rate, or BLEU scores) in a way that would allow comparative analysis. The exclusion criteria ruled out non-Indian
sign language studies unless they provided direct comparative baselines, hardware-based systems devoid of machine
learning components, hearing aid research, non-language gesture recognition, and studies not producing quantitative
results or lacking a significant theoretical contribution.
2.3. Data Extraction
The researchers collected data from each study, including information on dataset features, model design, and system
performance measurements such as accuracy, F1-score, Word Error Rate, and BLEU score, together with the testing
conditions (controlled and real-world testing) and the availability of both code and datasets. The two reviewers
conducted data extraction independently and resolved their differences through discussion until they reached agreement.
2.4. Quality Assessment Framework
The original purpose of QUADAS-2 was to evaluate diagnostic accuracy studies, but this review used it to examine
the research quality and bias of studies that applied deep learning for sign language interpretation. The four original
domains (patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow/timing) were reinterpreted to align with dataset
quality, model evaluation, reproducibility, and generalization.

A scoring system was developed to enable comparisons between different research studies, but the validation process
for this system remains incomplete, which introduces potential bias. The assessment of research quality used a revised
QUADAS-2 tool transformed into a 25-point scoring system that included five specific categories for deep learning
studies. The Methodological Rigor component assessed research design through three methods: ablation studies,
statistical testing, and hyperparameter control. The evaluation of technical contributions used a 0–5 scoring system,
where an “Excellent” rating of 5 points indicated more than 5% accuracy improvement over existing solutions or the
solution of new problems, “Good” received 4 points for a 3–5% accuracy gain, “Fair” received 3 points for a 1–3%
accuracy improvement, and “Poor” received 2 points for insufficient progress. The Result Reliability and Generalization
assessment measured results across different signers and environments to determine performance stability. The High
Quality group (≥ 18points, n = 30) demonstrated solid experimental design, provided all essential code and data,
achieved more than ten peer-reviewed citations, and proved its findings through real-world deployment and benchmark
establishment. The Medium Quality group (12–17 points,n = 12) applied valid research methods but did not provide
access to their code and data, while their signer collection showed limited diversity. The Lower Quality group (< 12
points, n = 5) raised concerns about their research methods but were retained to document particular findings about
rare regional languages. The authors included some studies from their own previous research, which they assessed
alongside other studies to maintain research objectivity.
3. Architectural Evolution (2020 to 2025)
The review included 47 studies in the final analysis following the PRISMA selection process. The recognition of
Indian Sign Language through deep learning methods progressed through four overlapping periods, each characterized
by a different paradigm that tackled specific technical limitations of the previous methods. The reported accuracy
ranges represent values observed across individual studies and datasets, rather than pooled or statistically aggregated
estimates. Examining the development timeline reveals the strengths and weaknesses of modern systems. The change
in architectural designs during the years 2020–2025 is depicted in Figure 3.
Figure 3: Architectural evolution in epochs.
3.1. Epoch I: CNN Dominance (2020 to 2022)
Early ISL recognition systems used CNNs through a transfer learning process that drew on ImageNet to obtain VGG-16
and ResNet-50 as initial models. Reported accuracy varies across studies depending on dataset size, model architecture,
and training strategy. Performance decreases when researchers use smaller datasets with limited fine-tuning, whereas it
improves through the combination of larger datasets and optimized transfer learning methods. The different testing
conditions lead to different results because they do not use a common testing standard. Multiple research studies
show that performance decreases when environmental conditions change, especially during lighting variations and
background clutter. The performance of a system therefore depends on two factors, namely the size of its dataset and
the success of its transfer learning techniques.

3.2. Epoch II: CNN-LSTM Hybrids (2022 to 2023)
CNN-LSTM frameworks unified convolutional spatial feature extraction with recurrent sequential motion modeling
through LSTMs [13], thus solving the problem of the temporal blindness of CNNs [14]. Waghmare et al. improved
CNN-only baselines by 12% using a combination of ResNet-50 and bidirectional LSTM [15]. In our previous work
[4], CNN-LSTM achieved 95.1% accuracy on the Kannada dataset under controlled conditions, while CNN-GRU
(94.8%) and 3D CNN (90.2%) achieved lower accuracy, with CNN-LSTM chosen for production deployment owing
to its accuracy–latency balance [4]. The reported accuracy for dynamic signs was in the range of 88–97% with GPU
inference latency of 60–120 milliseconds [15, 4]. However, critical limitations remained: generalization gaps exceeding
12% for unknown signers [16], the vanishing gradient problem for sequences longer than 50 frames, which required
gradient clipping [13], high computational costs [17, 18], and sequential processing bottlenecks that made real-time
performance impossible on resource-constrained devices [8, 19, 20].
3.3. Epoch III: Pose Estimation Revolution (2023 to 2024)
This paradigm shift led to a preference for two-stage approaches that separated pose estimation from classification.
Pose estimation models such as MediaPipe Holistic were responsible for the extraction of 543 landmarks representing
the hands, body, and face, after which these landmarks were handed over to trained classifiers for sign recognition. The
proportion of edge deployment rose from 5% in 2022 to 60% by 2024. Among the successful implementations were the
recognition of Marathi Sign Language on a Raspberry Pi 4 and research demonstrating how combining skin landmarks
with manual signals increased accuracy by 5.4%. The trade-offs included a 2–3% accuracy decrease under the best
controlled conditions, owing to the system’s inability to capture the subtleties of color and texture, while the approach
was also prone to occlusions of vital body parts that might lead to sign misinterpretation.
3.4. Epoch IV: Transformers (2024 to 2025)
The self-attention mechanisms of the transformer architectures allowed for parallel processing and also overcame
the issue of vanishing gradients for long sequences. The LiST model showed that sparse attention could reduce the
number of parameters by 45% while still offering comparable accuracy with the possibility of edge deployment. The
application of dual-stream vision transformers in the UMANG e-governance platform showed strong field performance,
as reflected in the “Good” System Usability Scores, but the average latency of 6.53 seconds was higher than the
set accessibility benchmarks. Critical challenges remained: very large training data requirements (10,000+ videos),
quadratic model complexity making the approach incompatible with long sequences, continuous processing time being
3–4 times longer than the simpler baseline CNN-LSTM, and difficulty in predicting the best deployment scenario given
the accuracy–efficiency trade-offs. The timeline of architectural evolution is summarized in Table 1.

4. Regional Sign Language Research Distribution
India’s nine significant regional sign languages refer to independent linguistic systems and not ISL dialects, as shown
by the 15–20% variation in their core vocabulary, distinct grammar, and separate morphology. The distribution of
research is very different from the locations of the speaker populations, which results in significant gaps in access. The
relationship between studies and speaker population is illustrated in Figure 4.
4.1. Kannada Sign Language (KSL)
KSL is used by about 3.3 million DHH people in Karnataka [21]. The research journey has shown gradual sophistication.
An initial study achieved 82% accuracy with a limited sign set using static recognition [6]. A thorough investigation
on 12,000 KSL videos (35 signs, 15 signers, various environments) compared three architectures: 3D CNN (90.2%),
CNN-GRU (94.8%), and CNN-LSTM (95.1%) [4, 21].

CNN-LSTM was chosen as the best for production deployment in KSL Medical Interpreter applications [21], demon-

4.2. Tamil Sign Language (TSL)
TSL exhibits linguistic features such as a subject-object-verb grammatical pattern, which contrasts with the topic-
comment organization of ISL. Gobhinath and Sophia obtained precise results in isolated sign recognition using a
graph neural network method [22]. Thillai Sivakavi and Minu created the Tamil Lang transformer neural text-to-sign
production system, which performs text-to-sign conversion [8]. Significant challenges still need to be addressed, such as
the incomplete transition from static to continuous recognition, the deficiency of cross-signer scenario assessment, and
the constraint of 1,000-word vocabulary models, which hinders use in areas such as medical and legal communication.
4.3. Marathi Sign Language (MSL)
MSL is likely to be used by 1.8 million DHH people [19] and has so far been researched very little, with just a handful
of peer-reviewed publications [19]. Efficient resource-edge models can run on low-cost devices such as the Raspberry Pi,
with eye-gaze input creating innovative accessibility features. Research is quite limited as a result of restricted data:
the largest available dataset has just 1,200 isolated signs, which is very small when compared with the data available for
the Gujarati language [23, 24]. Few studies have investigated continuous sign recognition, cross-signer generalization
testing, or large-scale real-world validation beyond initial pilot trials. A real-time translation of MSL into text and
speech has been created, showcasing the feasibility of an end-to-end accessibility pipeline. This research opens up
the possibility of practical deployments for the recognition of regional languages that would be able to produce not
only recognition output as speech and text but also the combination of speech synthesis with written text through
multimodal output generation.
4.4. Gujarati Sign Language (GSL)
The GIDSL dataset significantly advanced Gujarati Sign Language research: 118,000 meticulously annotated videos
from 25 different signers at various locations, the result of an 18-month collection effort that aimed to establish new
data quality standards [23, 24]. CNN-LSTM-based methods reached an isolated sign recognition accuracy of 89.6% [23],
and further study increased this to 94% while reducing the cross-signer generalization gap [24]. Nevertheless, accuracy
in continuous sentence recognition is only 81.7% [23], implying that fluent real-world translation lags considerably
behind isolated word classification. The technology’s capability in uncontrolled environments remains largely unproven
[23, 24].

4.5. Malayalam Sign Language (MSL)
The Aksharavaani detector integrated MediaPipe with LSTM and attained an accuracy of 89.7% on 35 alphabet
signs, along with a 15MB Android app deployment (System Usability Scale 73.5/100, 12 DHH participants) [25]. The
mapping of Malayalam sentences to ISL translation using a rule-based approach achieved 78.3% grammatical correctness.
The system is built to support approximately 800,000 users. Vengassery and colleagues created Aksharavaani as
a Malayalam Sign Language detector optimized for real-time mobile deployment, showing the practicality of edge
computing for recognizing regional languages [25]. Their research highlights the relevance of efficient architectural
design that enables deployment on resource-limited devices while still achieving a level of accuracy suitable for practical
accessibility applications.
4.6. Bangla Sign Language (BdSL)
The BDSL49 dataset, which consists of 23,040 images, is published under a CC BY 4.0 open license with 4 signers and
Cohen’s κ = 0.94inter-annotator agreement [26, 27]. The combined VGG-16/ResNet-50 ensemble achieved a notable
98.23% test accuracy and 95.7% cross-signer accuracy [26]. Extensive research outlined specific CNN architectures
for Bangla sign language recognition, meeting the needs of Bengali-speaking deaf communities through systematic
architectural design focused on balancing accuracy with deployment constraints [28]. This project, in collaboration
with border regions, has already started to contribute to cross-border accessibility initiatives between the Indian and
Bangladeshi deaf communities by highlighting the linguistic and cultural connections between them [27].
4.7. Under-Studied Languages
Telugu Sign Language:With a dataset comprising only two studies that include only 500 videos, the Indian states
of Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, which are home to 8.1 million Telugu speakers, are quite poorly covered. One study
led by Ramanujam et al. developed a model that recognized 40 signs with an accuracy of 84.5% by combining CNN
and LSTM in a pilot study conducted among 120 DHH students in three Hyderabad schools. The critical gaps in this
area include the absence of continuous recognition research, medical and legal domain datasets, and dialectal variation
studies.
Assamese Sign Language:Studies focusing on the sign language of the Northeast Indian region addressed an overall
population of 1.4 million Assamese speakers. Bora et al. developed an Android app based on MediaPipe that reached
89.2% accuracy on 450 phrases with 50 users. One of the main problems is that the Northeast has a degree of linguistic
isolation, which leads to the production of distinct sign variants, along with limited research infrastructure and a lack
of connection with Tibeto-Burman sign language systems.
Punjabi Sign Language:The study conducted by Singh et al., addressing 2.8 million Punjabi speakers in India and
overseas, focused on creating a rule-based Punjabi-to-ISL animation system for 500 sentences without using any neural
recognition components. Cross-border collaboration on research into Pakistan Sign Language remains unexplored. The

5. Benchmark Datasets and Infrastructure
ISL recognition progress relies mainly on the availability of high-quality datasets, the use of powerful computational
infrastructure, and the validation of performance through real-world deployment. The recognized resources span three
national benchmarks (INCLUDE and CISLR), two regional datasets (GIDSL and BDSL49), and one domain-specific
resource (KSL Medical).

5.1. National Benchmark Datasets
INCLUDE is a corpus containing 270,000 frames that researchers use to study isolated sign recognition; its value
comes from 20 different signers who performed signs at multiple locations, which establishes a strong basis for testing
generalization, although it lacks continuous sign sequences. The CISLR dataset functions as a structured collection
containing sign instances that researchers have annotated at the word level and organized into sequences that enable
sentence-level analysis. It contains approximately 4,700 lexical items and is frequently used to assess both isolated and
continuous sign language recognition models. However, its controlled acquisition setting and limited signer diversity
should be considered when interpreting performance results.
5.2. Regional and Domain-Specific Resources
The GIDSL dataset for Gujarati is the largest and most detailed regional dataset, consisting of 118,000 videos with
annotations for both isolated and continuous sign recognition. ISLRTC maintains an ISL dictionary of 10,000 terms
that serves as a lexical resource fundamental to the whole field. The KSL Medical dataset targets health-related signs
that channel clinical communication systems while providing ethical protections for privacy. The costly nature of
dataset development is evidenced by GIDSL, which involved an 18-month collection protocol using a three-camera
setup across controlled, home, and outdoor environments, together with a two-stage annotation process that resulted
in inter-annotator agreement ofκ≥0.85.
5.3. Government Infrastructure and Digital Platforms
ISLRTC functions as a national regulatory body responsible for ISL approval, the maintenance of a dictionary of 10,000
terms, and the funding of research and development through a budget of INR 12 crore (2021–2024). This allocation
shows a preference for technology over the development of human resources, thus exposing possible barriers to the
implementation of accessibility.
5.4. Production Deployment: UMANG Platform
Assessment of the UMANG platform indicates a strong negative relationship between the complexity of the queries and
the accuracy of the system. Simple queries, which account for 45% of the interactions (for example, “train status”),
have an accuracy rate of 97.5%, while moderate-complexity queries that make up 40% of the queries have a 92.3%
accuracy rate, and the complex queries that account for 15% of the queries drop to 85.1%. The main improvements to
be made are to reduce the 7.7% misclassification rate, lower the 6.53-second average response latency (which is above
the sub-5-second accessibility benchmark), and provide services that go beyond the current railway and blood bank
integration.
6. Performance Analysis and Generalization
The complete performance assessment reveals essential details about accuracy development, ongoing system limitations,
and the architectural design choices that determine deployment success in real environments. The results from various
studies should be interpreted with caution because they involve different accuracy assessment methods. The performance
results depend heavily on the dataset attributes, which include size, signer diversity, and vocabulary difficulty, as well
as the evaluation methods. The absence of standardized benchmarks hinders accurate accuracy comparisons between
datasets because these comparisons do not fully reflect model superiority. The quantitative ranges reported in this
section are based on values observed across multiple studies and should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than
results derived from a formal meta-analysis.
6.1. Accuracy Evolution Across Epochs
The trajectory of sign language recognition has increased significantly in accuracy owing to the combination of new
architectures and innovation in datasets. Isolated recognition based on CNNs (2020 to 2022) reached an accuracy
of 79–92% on datasets usually consisting of fewer than 5,000 images, so the difference in accuracy mainly depended
on dataset size and transfer learning. In 2021, CNN ensemble architectures together with larger training datasets
(about 10,000 samples) raised performance to 85–95%. Concurrently, the use of transfer learning with fine-tuned CNN
backbones (VGG-16, ResNet-50) on datasets exceeding 20,000 samples led to accuracy of 92–98% in 2022. The following
year, 2023, saw the introduction of pose-based techniques that made use of MediaPipe and OpenPose, which gave nearly

the same accuracy (94–98%) but with considerably reduced computational latency, often providing 3–5 times faster
inference with little performance loss. Newer transformer-based techniques emerging in 2024 relied on larger datasets
and achieved an accuracy of 95–99%. The development of multimodal transformer models that combined visual,
skeletal, and linguistic cues achieved 96–99.5% accuracy by 2025, approaching the limits of human-level recognition
accuracy.
6.2. Cross-Signer Generalization
Cross-signer generalization research faces a primary obstacle because models show substantial performance declines
during cross-signer testing across multiple studies. The systems demonstrate difficulty when attempting to operate
under conditions that differ from their training environment. Models that reached 94% accuracy with familiar signers
experience a performance decline to 76% accuracy when tested with signers who maintain rapid execution and come
from various geographic areas. The strategies for reducing the impact of the problem include collecting 30+ different
signers, employing data augmentation to imitate variations, and using domain adaptation and meta-learning techniques
that give a 4–8% performance increase. The best-performing systems achieved a generalization gap as low as 2.5%
to withheld signers, outperforming the 12–20% declines found in current research, through effective signer diversity
management and advanced adaptation methods.
6.3. Environmental Robustness
The environmental factors that create different conditions across deployments substantially determine how well models
perform their tasks. Systems that operate under studio-controlled lighting conditions achieve 95–98% accuracy, but
their results drop when they enter conditions that lack sufficient light. The actual performance of models drops
significantly when researchers assess their capabilities in authentic testing environments that contain different lighting
conditions, background elements, and camera angles. The combination of poor lighting, complex backgrounds, and
off-angle signing results in severe accuracy loss reaching 30–50%. The development of environmentally robust systems
needs to proceed through strategies that require comprehensive data augmentation, including environmental variation
simulation.
6.4. Continuous Recognition Performance Gap
The simulation-to-reality gap is widest in continuous sign recognition, which enables complete phrase translation
without requiring any segmentation. Three interconnected problems are the reasons for this gap: co-articulation
effects, where one sign affects the next and transitional frames confuse even human annotators; temporal segmentation
difficulties arising from variable sign duration that lacks clear boundaries, so that fixed-length window models either
truncate or split signs incorrectly; and the absence of non-manual signals (facial expressions, head movements) that
constitute 25–30% of grammatical meaning. These problems do not exist separately; on the contrary, co-articulation is
the main cause of the temporal segmentation difficulty, and the lack of non-manual features prevents the models from
resolving the ambiguities that these signals clear up.
6.5. Computational Performance and Cost-Accuracy Trade-Offs
The selection of architecture for sign language recognition is a decision in which factors such as accuracy, latency, and
computational cost are all considered. Pose-based methods with LSTMs provide a good compromise between the
extremes: real-time system performance (43–80ms on a high-end GPU), minimal memory usage (20–45MB), and the
ability to run on a CPU-only edge device for smartphones. The transformer architectures, which often result in higher
accuracy, are nevertheless 2–3 times slower in processing speed and also require 150–400MB of memory, hence the
need for significant and expensive powerful hardware. Adaptive inference strategies dynamically route tasks such that
lightweight Pose+LSTM models operate at the edge for real-time interaction while heavy transformers perform cloud
batch processing, thus attaining a performance–economy balance. The performance comparison is provided in Table 3.

7.1. Continuous Sign Recognition
Continuous sign language recognition, which is the most critical and the most relevant to real life, is to be evaluated
against three major interrelated challenges that go beyond isolated word classification. The main problem comes from
continuity effects, where overlapping signs and transitional frames create confusion even for human annotators. The
second problem is that of temporal segmentation without fixed-length windows, which remains unresolved since sign
durations vary considerably with no clear boundaries. The third issue is that non-manual signals (facial expressions,
head movements), which constitute about 25–30% of the linguistic information, remain poorly integrated.
7.2. Data Scarcity for Regional Languages
The most significant limitation in the development of regional language systems is the lack of sufficient data. The
quantity of ASL datasets exceeds 500 video-hours, whereas ISL has only about 170 hours, a three-fold reduction.
Regional variants usually possess only 3–6 hours for one language, which is 10–50 times less than the 60–150 hours
necessary for convergence. A total of 600–1,500 hours of video is needed for full coverage of ten regional languages.
To overcome data scarcity, it is necessary to apply a variety of methods that integrate transfer learning, data
augmentation, and active learning approaches. The accuracy achieved by transfer learning is 85% with only 500 samples
from the target, versus 65% with supervised learning alone, thus reducing the need for labeled data. One advanced
data augmentation technique, together with others mentioned in a comprehensive survey, gives only a 3–7% accuracy
improvement through spatial transformations, temporal modifications, and the generation of synthetic data.
7.3. Multi-Modal Integration Deficit
A substantial 70% of the prepared systems take manual signs only into consideration, leaving a large integration gap.
Only 5% of the systems give complete integration to non-manual signals despite their grammatical, lexical, and prosodic
significance. Among the technical problems are feature extraction from hundreds of facial keypoints and the alignment
of manual signs across different gesture durations.
The classification of vision-based hand gesture recognition methods for human-computer interaction systems has been
presented, outlining key aspects of comprehensive multimodal approaches. The authors report that a strong gesture
recognition system needs the simultaneous processing of hand movements, facial expressions, and body actions.
7.4. Generalization Challenges
Robust generalization has posed a great challenge over the years, characterized by several aspects. Recognition of
unknown signers may result in a drop of 5–18% in accuracy owing to variation in signing styles, hand shapes, and
speeds, all of which can be addressed with 30–50+ signer datasets featuring diverse characteristics, together with
adversarial learning in conjunction with meta-learning methods. Table 4 offers a comprehensive overview of the
challenges encountered and their quantified impact on performance.
Performance degradation of as much as 15% is experienced when the domain shifts from laboratory-controlled to
real-world scenarios, which is handled through the techniques of domain randomization and test-time adaptation.
Long-tail distribution issues are considered among the primary generalization challenges, where the recognition of rare
signs comes with an accuracy of only 70–85% compared with 95–98% for common signs. Darekar et al. presented a
system for the recognition of Marathi sign language for physically disabled people, achieving this by implementing
strategies of robust generalization through the use of balanced training data and adversarial augmentation [31].

8. Future Research Directions
Addressing the identified obstacles and gaps requires strategic methodological innovations and impact-based planning
across 2025–2030.
8.1. Self-Supervised Learning
Self-supervised learning significantly improves performance in addressing the lack of data by leveraging existing
unlabeled data to require fewer than 70% of the labeled data otherwise needed, reducing labeling requirements by
30–50%. Masked frame prediction drives the models to reconstruct absent frames, thus gaining knowledge of the
temporal and spatial dynamics (+25% performance gain). Contrastive learning provides a way for the models to learn
how to tell signs apart using different kinds of augmentations, thus achieving a +20% improvement. Pseudo-labeling is
an approach in which training is continuously informed by labels generated by the model (+15% improvement where
the supervision is tight).
8.2. Cross-Lingual Transfer Learning
The similarities among various Indian sign languages help to make cross-lingual transfer possible, which is a major tool
for accelerating development. A simple pre-training on ISL and fine-tuning on a specific region reached 85% accuracy
with 500 target samples, which is better than the 78% achieved from training from scratch. Advanced adversarial
domain adaptation helps to reduce distribution differences. Multilingual joint training carried out with a common
encoder and separate prediction heads for each language allows for zero-shot recognition through the use of phonological
decomposition and semantic embeddings.
8.3. Linguistically-Informed Architectures
Using language-based models instead of purely data-driven ones is a justification for sustained precision and performance.
Phonological decomposition represents the constituent parts of signs (about 50 handshapes× 30 locations × 20
movements × 8 orientations) instead of treating them as distinct groups, and this even allows generalization to unseen
signs.

The rules applicable to ISL (for example, topic-comment structure) can be treated as either hard or trainable constraints
that enhance the quality of continuous translation. Morphological modeling treats verb direction inflections and
aspectual changes as integral parts of the main model.
8.4. ISLR-Bench Standardization Framework
Lack of standardization leads to a situation where the findings of studies lack direct comparability. We propose the
ISLR-Bench as a conceptual framework for improving standardization in future ISL research. The first track, the
Isolated Sign Recognition Track, measures accuracy on known signers and the critical unknown-signer generalization
gap using the INCLUDE dataset. The Continuous Sign Recognition Track conducts Word Error Rate calculation in
both controlled and in-the-wild environments using the CISLR dataset. The Regional Language Robustness Track
suggests evaluating two regional variants in terms of cross-lingual transfer efficiency. The Real-Time Deployment
Assessment Track reports latency and memory usage across various hardware, including desktop GPUs and mobile
CPUs.
8.5. Five-Year Implementation Roadmap (2026 to 2030)
We propose a phased roadmap that could guide the translation of research into real-world impact. The first phase
(2025–2026), Foundational Data and Benchmarks, creates a national ISL dataset collected through ethical methods
and provides resources for five primary regional languages that DHH teams continuously monitor while generating
public benchmarks and maintaining ongoing leaderboards. The second phase (2026–2027), Algorithmic Breakthroughs,
aims at human-level recognition in controlled settings, demonstrating more than 99% isolated sign accuracy and less
than 5% word error rate for continuous ISL performance. This phase creates advanced open-source models that come
with specific constraints on processing speed and model size. The third phase (2027–2028), Real-World Robustness,
operates extensive pilot programs across 15 states that test the systems with more than 10,000 DHH users while using
unidentified signers and extreme weather to assess system performance. The fourth phase (2028–2029), Pervasive
Deployment, enables complete real-time ISL recognition across consumer devices, achieving sub-50ms latency on
mid-range smartphones. The ISL Mobile SDK enables the creation of 500+ applications that will serve more than
1 million users. The fifth phase (2029–2030), Ecosystem Maturity and Equity, delivers complete public access to
government digital services operating as official digital platforms, while evaluating success through three specific
metrics: 15–25% employment growth for DHH workers, 70% literacy achievement, and the development of a sustainable
community-based technology sector.
9. Synthesis and Discussion
This systematic review documents the complete progress of Indian Sign Language recognition technology that developed
from 2020 to 2025, showing both major advances and ongoing challenges that prevent equitable implementation.
9.1. Persistent Barriers to Equitable Implementation
The current development of technology shows rapid progress, yet existing barriers still prevent people from accessing
its full benefits. The continuous recognition process demonstrates an 8–15% performance decrease in actual situations
because of co-articulation, temporal segmentation, and the addition of non-manual signals that include facial expressions
and body posture. Multiple studies demonstrate that these signals play vital roles in understanding sign language. The
regional language development process suffers from data availability problems because indigenous languages exist in
datasets only 10–100 times smaller than ISL and at least 10 times smaller than ASL resources, which results from cost,
coordination, and ethical restrictions. The issue of generalization is still present: for unknown signers there is a drop in
accuracy of 5–18%, and a change in environment produces degradation of 8–15%, which indicates the necessity for
diverse datasets and sophisticated training methods.
9.2. Stakeholder Call to Action
We recommend that stakeholders work together to support equitable ISL technology development. Researchers should
prioritize real-world challenges, particularly continuous recognition, and ensure that evaluation includes unknown
signers. They should also release code and models within six months of publication and promote the meaningful
inclusion of DHH contributors, ensuring the presence of at least 50% DHH co-authorship.

Government bodies could consider setting up an allocation of INR 100 crore for the development of datasets, in-
frastructure, and innovation in 2026–2030; requiring all digital platforms to support ISL by 2028 with penalties for
non-compliance; and creating a National ISL Data Commons.
9.3. Vision for 2030
We envisage that by 2030, ISL technologies could significantly enhance innovation and digital accessibility. Indian Sign
Language and more than ten regional languages will be translated in real time with very good accuracy everywhere and
on any device for both public and private usage. Technological barriers will diminish, which in turn will lead to growth
in DHH community participation across different sectors such as employment (+15–25%), education (+10–15%), and
healthcare access (+25–30%). The country’s linguistic diversity will also be documented and acknowledged.
9.4. Technology As Bridge, Not Replacement
Technology should be regarded as an access enabler, not as a human replacement. It offers users empowerment through
wide, constant, low-cost daily communication access. Still, it is not able to comprehend cultural intricacy, show
emotional compassion, or make moral decisions like humans, particularly in delicate fields such as law or mental health.
The future is hybrid, where technology takes over routine, high-volume interactions, allowing human professionals to
deal with the delicate and complex communications, and where both roles are equally valued and rewarded socially.
9.5. Methodological Limitations
This review has several methodological limitations. Although a structured PRISMA approach was followed, the
adaptation of QUADAS-2 into a quantitative scoring system is not formally validated and may introduce subjectivity.
The study achieved broad database coverage yet may not have found all relevant studies because of database indexing
limitations. The different research methods used in the included studies, together with the various assessment techniques
employed to evaluate results, create barriers that make it difficult to directly compare the performance evaluation
results across studies.
10. Conclusion
This systematic review has detailed the technical maturity and application potential of Indian Sign Language recognition
from 2020 to 2025. The field shows progress through architectural evolution that started with CNNs (79–92%), moved
to pose estimation (89–94%, 5–7× faster), and finally reached transformers (91–98% controlled, 81–90% real-world).
The research has achieved substantial progress through its investigation of nine regional languages, which have been
implemented in both healthcare and e-governance systems. The primary problems persist because an 8–15% performance
loss occurs during continuous recognition, unknown-signer accuracy drops between 5–18%, and regional variant data
remains 10–100 times scarcer. The desired results will depend on collaborative work between researchers, policymakers,
industry experts, and members of the DHH community. The technological opportunity for truly equitable accessibility
is not only a matter of debate but also one of social responsibility. To realize this vision, the collective will must be
strong enough to build socially the strongest technical foundations, benefiting millions of DHH people in India.
