## Abstract

Sign language relies on visual gestures of human body parts to convey meaning and plays a vital role in modern society to communicate and interact with people having hearing difficulty as well as for human–machine interaction applications. This field has attracted a growing attention in recent years and several research outcomes have been witnessed covering various issues including sign acquisition, segmentation, recognition, translation and linguistic structures. In this paper, a comprehensive up-to-date survey of the state-of-the-art literature of automated sign language processing is presented. The survey provides a taxonomy and review of the body of knowledge and research efforts with focus on acquisition devices, available databases, and recognition techniques for fingerspelling signs, isolated sign words, and continuous sentence recognition systems. It covers recent advances including deep machine learning and multimodal approaches and discusses various related challenges. This survey is directed to junior researchers and industry developers working on sign language gesture recognition and related systems to gain insights and identify distinctive aspects and current status of existing landscape as well as future perspectives leading to further advancements.

## Introduction

Communication plays an essential role with tremendous effects on human life, e.g. gain and exchange knowledge, interact and develop social relationships, and express feelings and needs. While spoken language is the normal way for people to communicate, sign language (SL) represents a main alternative for people with auditory or vocal impairment or difficulty. Recently, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared that more than 400 million people worldwide have disabling hearing loss which forms about 5% of the world’s population.^{1} Hearing impairment may range from mild (20 dB−40 dB below normal hearing threshold) to profound (more than 81 dB below normal hearing threshold) and can be caused as a result of a birth defect or an illness, or develop at a later stage of age. With the recent advances in information technology and robotics, sign gestures is not only important to overcome communication barriers between humans but also for human–machine interaction (Ryumin et al., 2019, Homburg et al., 2019) and translation of sign language into another form of natural language and vice versa (Dutta et al., 2015, Rajaganapathy et al., 2015).

Though there are some references to a sign language that dates back to the 5th century BC, it was not until the past centuries where some attempts have been made to develop a system for deaf and dump communication within their community and to other people. For instance, an early attempt in western countries in 16th century attributed to a Spanish monk, Pedro Ponce de León, as the first teacher for the deaf who described a manual alphabet for fingerspelling, formed the base for Juan Pablo Bonet’s writings in 1620 for teaching deaf people. The first free school for deaf was founded in Paris in 1755 and the first American School for deaf was founded in Connecticut in 1817. Almost in parallel, the British sign language has evolved and widely varied from the American sign language. However, a resolution was passed in Milan in 1880 at the International Congress on Education of the Deaf to officially prohibit using sign language in favor of speech. Not until the past two decades, some countries such as UK have recognized the sign language as a language in its own right and become part of the formal educational system rather than charity boards. Similar to natural languages, sign language is not universal, i.e. it is geographically specific and more than 135 variants exist around the world (Luqman et al., 2017, Makhashen et al., 2019). Examples of popular sign languages include Spanish (Lengua de Signos Española or LSE), American (ASL), British (BSL), Irish (ISL), French (langue des signes française or LSF), Australian (Auslan), Chinese (CSL), Dutch (Nederlandse Gebarentaal or NGT), German (Deutsche Gebärdensprache or DGS)), Brazilian (Libras), Russian (RSL) and Arabic (ArSL). Moreover, some countries may share the same sign language alphabet, e.g. British, Australian and New Zealand (BANZSL), while others can have different sign languages albeit sharing the same spoken language, e.g. BSL and ASL.

Sign language is considered as a spontaneous non-verbal way for people to communicate through manual and/or non-manual body gestures for visual signing to convey meaning. Manual gestures are the primary components of a sign language and usually involve hand movements to express the signer’s emotions and thoughts (Cheok et al., 2019). Manual gestures are naturally accompanied with simultaneous non-manual gestures such as head movements, facial expressions and body postures (Nair and Bindu, 2013). Facial expressions employ different face parts such as eye, eyebrow, forehead, and mouth to express or stress meanings that cannot be revealed by manual gestures. Facial expressions are a central focal point to mark syntactical and grammatical information in sign languages such as conditions and questions. For instance, they can be used to express negation or serve as adverbs and adjectives of manual signs distinguishing between signs with similar hand gestures (Goldstein and Feldman, 1996). An example is shown in Fig. 1 where facial expressions are used to reflect the emotion of two ArSL signs: “Beautiful” and “Ugly”. Each sign in the first group shares the face posture with a corresponding similar sign in the second group. Moreover, head motion is an important component of the non-manual articulators of a sign language that can be integrated with manual gestures or used as an independent sign. Fig. 2 shows an illustrative example of a sign that uses hand and head postures together with eye closing to express the “Sleep” sign in ArSL. Lip motion is another popular form of non-manual gestures for voiced and unvoiced speech recognition but recently is gaining increasing attention for sign language recognition (Koller et al., 2015b). Sign gestures can be classified differently into static or dynamic gestures. The former type keeps the position of the body part fixed during signing and typically depends on the orientation and shape of hands and fingers for signing digits and alphabets (Pisharady and Saerbeck, 2015). In contrast, dynamic gestures depend on hands and body motion during signing and this type of gestures is used mainly in signing words and sentences (Luqman and Mahmoud, 2019).

Automated Sign Language Recognition (SLR) continues to gain interest within the research community and in recent years there has been an influx of related publications. While a number of surveys and reviews were published, they mostly focus on specific issues and the continuous development of the field necessitates a more up-to-date literature survey. This paper aims at providing a comprehensive survey and taxonomy for the state-of-the-art sign language recognition. It explores various aspects and techniques related to sign language. Furthermore, it covers latest advances including deep learning and reviews most common resources available for sign language recognition. It describes publicly available datasets used in the related research. Moreover, it discusses existing challenges and highlights potential research directions for sign language recognition. This survey is directed to junior researchers and industry developers in related fields to gain key insights of sign language recognition and related human–machine interaction systems. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 provides an overview and reviews related works. Section 3 describes different sign acquisition devices while Section 4 lists available datasets for sign language recognition. Section 5 extensively discusses different techniques adopted for sign language recognition. Finally, conclusions are directions for advancing the field are outlined in Section 6.

Sign language is a form of visual communication employing hand gestures, body movements, and facial expressions. The growing prevalence of hearing impairment has driven the research community towards the domain of Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR), which involves identification of successive signs in a video stream without prior knowledge of temporal boundaries. This survey article conducts a review of CSLR research, spanning the past 25 years, offering insights into the evolution of CSLR systems. A critical analysis of 126 studies is presented and organized into a taxonomy comprising seven critical dimensions: sign language, data acquisition, input modality, sign language cues, recognition techniques, utilized datasets, and overall performance. Additionally, the article investigated the classification of deep-learning CSLR models, categorizing them based on spatial, temporal, and alignment methods, while identifying their advantages and limitations. The article also explored various research aspects including CSLR challenges, the significance of non-manual features in CSLR systems, and identified gaps in existing literature. This literature taxonomy serves as a resource aiding researchers in the development and positioning of novel CSLR techniques. The study emphasizes the efficacy of multi-modal deep learning systems in capturing diverse sign language cues. However, the examination of existing research uncovers numerous limitations, calling for continued research and innovation within the CSLR domain. The findings not only contribute to the broader understanding of sign language recognition but also lay the foundations for future research initiatives aimed at addressing the persistent challenges within this emerging field.

## Abstract:

The paper reviews the progress in Sinhala Sign Language recognition (SSL) over the last decade, tracing the techniques from early image processing to advanced deep learning models, indicating methodologies involved, datasets used, and challenges. The paper shows that the evolution has progressed from basic classifiers to sophisticated machine learning techniques that integrate CNNs, RNNs, and transformer architectures. It also presents the implementation of multimodal systems using hand gestures, facial expressions, and body postures. The review identifies the limitations of current recognition in SSL, such as scarce complete datasets, technical difficulties, and a lack of robust continuous recognition. Finally, it points out future research directions, stressing that further work must focus on growing datasets, making lightweight models for edge devices, and collaboration for further growth in SSL recognition technology.

## Sign acquisition devices

Acquisition techniques of sign language can be classified into sensor-based, vision-based, and a combination of the two (Ahmed et al., 2018). Sensor-based acquisition techniques depend on a set of sensors to record the signers’ gesture information (Ahmed et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2019b) while vision-based techniques use video cameras to capture signers’ gesture (Pisharady and Saerbeck, 2015).

Multi-modal Deep Learning Approach to Improve Sentence level Sinhala Sign Language Recognition

H.H.S.N.Haputhanthri, H.M.N.Tennakoon, M.A.S.M.Wijesekara,  
B.H.R.Pushpananda, H.N.D. Thilini

University of Colombo School of Computing,

Sign language is used across the world for communication purposes within hearing-impaired communities. Hearing people are not well versed in sign language and most hearing-impaired are not good in general text, creating a communication barrier. Research on Sign Language Recognition (SLR) systems have shown admirable solutions for this issue. In Sri Lanka, machine learning along with neural networks has been the prominent domain of research in Sinhala SLR. All previous research is mainly focused on word-level SLR using hand gestures for translation. While this works for a certain vocabulary, there are many signs interpreted through other spatial cues like lip movements and facial expressions. Therefore, translation is limited and sometimes the interpretations can be misleading. In this research, we propose a multi-modal Deep Learning approach that can effectively recognize sentence-level sign gestures using hand and lip movements and translate to Sinhala text. The model consists of modules for visual feature extraction (ResNet), contextual relationship modeling (transformer encoder with multi-head attention), alignment (CTC) and decoding (Prefix beam search). A dataset consisting 22 of sentences used for evaluations was collected under controlled conditions for a specific day-to-day scenario (a conversation between a vendor and a customer in a shop). The proposed model achieves a best Word Error Rate (WER) of 12.70 on the testing split, improving over the single-stream model which shows a best WER of 17.41, suggesting a multi-modal approach improves overall SLR.

Keywords: Sign language, Sinhala Sign Language, Continuous Sign Language Recognition, Deep Learning, Multi-modal fusion
