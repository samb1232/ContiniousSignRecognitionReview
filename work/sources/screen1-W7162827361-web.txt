L1215@P40: 4.9.1 Isolated Sign Language Recognition (ISLR)
L1216@P40: WLASL [9] and MS-ASL [19] are the most frequently referenced benchmarks for wordlevel recognition, appearing in numerous cross-dataset studies. Reported performance
L1217@P40: results indicate significant challenges in scaling vocabulary: the best Inflated 3D
L1218@P40: Convolutional (I3D) model achieved 65.89% Top-1 accuracy on the WLASL100
L1219@P40: subset, but only 32.48% on the full WLASL2000 subset [9]. Regional datasets provide
L1220@P40: essential ISLR baselines for under-represented languages. For example, BdSLW60 [29]
L1221@P40: for Bangla reports 97.0% accuracy using Support Vector Machine (SVM) on a flipped
L1222@P40: dataset, while AUTSL [13] for Turkish achieved 95.95% accuracy under random
L1223@P40: splits and 62.02% under signer-independent protocols. Specialized datasets such as
L1224@P40: LIBRAS-UFOP [43] (Brazilian Sign Language) and Alabib-65 [37] (Algerian Sign
L1225@P40: Language) focus on model precision using minimal-pairs vocabularies and subtle
L1226@P40: sign classes, reporting recognition rates of 74.25% and multi-method comparative
L1227@P40: baselines, respectively. Recent datasets such as RoCoISLR [28] (Romanian) and
L1228@P40: CASL-W60 [41] (Central African) utilize VideoMAE and transformer-based models
L1229@P40-41: to establish standardized evaluation foundations for their respective regions.42
L1230@P41: 4.9.2 Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR)
L1231@P41: RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather 2014 [23] remains the primary benchmark for continuous
L1232@P41: sign language recognition, reporting a 45.0% Word Error Rate (WER) on single-signer
L1233@P41: setups as a baseline for upper-body tracking evaluation. SIGNUM [24] serves as a
L1234@P41: foundational benchmark for signer-independent continuous recognition, encompassing
L1235@P41: a vocabulary of 450 basic signs in German Sign Language. More recent datasets, such
L1236@P41: as CE-CNSL [31] (Chinese Sign Language), address the transition from laboratory
L1237@P41: settings to real-life scenarios, reporting an 18.6% WER using the TFNet time-frequency
L1238@P41: model. KSL-Guide [42] contributes by incorporating interrogative sentences into its
L1239@P41: training corpus; the authors report that this inclusion can nearly halve the WER for
L1240@P41: continuous recognition tasks in real-world application environments.
L1241@P41: 4.9.3 Sign Language Translation (SLT)
L1242@P41: Large-scale multimodal datasets such as How2Sign [18] facilitate continuous ASL-toEnglish translation across more than 35,000 signed sentences and an English vocabulary
L1243@P41: of 16,000 words. CSL-Daily [32] introduced the Sign Back-Translation (SignBT)
L1244@P41: method for Chinese Sign Language translation, achieving a Bilingual Evaluation
L1245@P41: Understudy (BLEU) score of 21.34 by leveraging external monolingual data. BLEU
L1246@P41: measures the degree of overlap between machine-generated translations and human
L1247@P41: reference translations, with higher scores indicating closer agreement. MediapiRGB [38] offers the largest French Sign Language translation corpus, comprising 86
L1248@P41: hours of journalistic content. Web-mined corpora such as YouTube-ASL [20] and
L1249@P41: OpenASL [21] significantly expand the scale of translation tasks. YouTube-ASL [20],
L1250@P41: for example, includes approximately 1,000 hours of video and more than 2,500 unique
L1251@P41: signers, and reports a state-of-the-art fine-tuned baseline of 12.39 BLEU on the
L1252@P41: How2Sign benchmark.
L1253@P41: 4.9.4 Specialized and Emerging Research Tasks
L1254@P41: Several datasets address applications beyond general recognition tasks. BOBSL [22]
L1255@P41: supports large-scale sign language alignment and sign-spotting, reporting baseline
L1256@P41: results for subtitle alignment across diverse broadcast topics. Educational applications
L1257@P41: are central to SMILE [33] (Swiss German Sign Language) and SL-ReDu GSL [10]
L1258@P41: (Greek Sign Language), both designed for second-language (L2) learner assessment at
L1259@P41: the CEFR A1 level and which provide tools for student self-monitoring and automated
L1260@P41: feedback. Domain-specific communication is addressed by KSU-SSL [36], the

L1392@P45: 5.2.4 Incorporate Non-Manual Feature Annotations
L1393@P45: Future datasets should include annotation of non-manual features such as facial expressions, eyebrow position, mouthing, and head movements, which convey grammatical
L1394@P45: meaning in sign languages. As only 3 of the 30 mapped datasets fully annotate these
L1395@P45: features, there is a significant opportunity for new releases to address this gap. Tools
L1396@P45: such as ELAN and OpenFace can facilitate this process, and retrospective annotation
L1397@P45: of existing datasets is technically feasible.
L1398@P45: 5.2.5 Report Total Duration Consistently
L1399@P45: All dataset documentation should report total duration in hours or minutes, calculated
L1400@P45: from frame counts and frame rates if direct measurement is unavailable. This practice
L1401@P45: is standard in comparable video recognition benchmarks and should be considered a
L1402@P45-46: minimum requirement for sign language datasets.47
L1403@P46: 5.2.6 Expand Geographic and Linguistic Coverage
L1404@P46: Future dataset development should prioritize sign languages currently absent from
L1405@P46: the research literature, including those from Sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia,
L1406@P46: Central Asia, and the Middle East beyond Saudi Arabia. Coordinated international
L1407@P46: initiatives in close collaboration with local deaf communities are most likely to
L1408@P46: yield representative and linguistically accurate datasets. Bragg et al. [1] specifically
L1409@P46: recommend community-centered approaches to dataset design to address the structural
L1410@P46: inequalities evident in the current distribution.
L1411@P46: 5.3 Task-Based Dataset Usage Guidelines
L1412@P46: This section offers a structured guide for dataset selection based on the computational
L1413@P46: task a researcher intends to pursue. Recommendations are based on annotation,
L1414@P46: modality, and signer pool characteristics recorded in the extraction spreadsheet.
L1415@P46: Table 9 presents a concise overview, and the following sub-sections discuss each task
L1416@P46: type in detail.
L1417@P46: 5.3.1 Isolated Sign Language Recognition
L1418@P46: For isolated sign language recognition (ISLR), researchers should select datasets that
L1419@P46: provide gloss labels and a published signer-independent split. WLASL [9] and MSASL [19] are recommended for large-vocabulary ASL recognition. AUTSL [13] and
L1420@P46: BosphorusSign22k [25] are suitable for Turkish Sign Language with RGB and depth
L1421@P46: modalities. MM-WLAuslan [34] is the preferred choice for Australian Sign Language,
L1422@P46: offering over 282,900 samples with a multi-view configuration. Slovo [26] provides the
L1423@P46: most subject-diverse Russian Sign Language resource, with 194 signers across 1,000
L1424@P46: classes. For under-resourced languages, INCLUDE [30], Alabib-65 [37], RoCoISLR
L1425@P46: [28], SignIT [40], and CASL-W60 [41] are the only available benchmarks for Indian,
L1426@P46: Algerian, Romanian, Italian, and Central African Sign Languages, respectively. It
L1427@P46: should be noted that KSU-SSL [36], INCLUDE [30], and RoCoISLR [28] do not
L1428@P46: define signer-independent splits, which limits the rigor of evaluations on these datasets.
L1429@P46: 5.3.2 Continuous Sign Language Recognition
L1430@P46: Continuous sign language recognition (CSLR) requires temporal gloss annotations,
L1431@P46: time-stamped labels marking the start and end boundaries of each sign within a
L1432@P46: continuous video sequence, and a signer-independent split. RWTH-PHOENIXWeather 2014 [23] remains the most widely used CSLR benchmark, with 1,558
L1433@P46: glosses, 9 signers, a standardized split, and paired sentence translations. SIGNUM [24]
L1434@P46: provides a controlled German Sign Language benchmark with 450 signs from 25
L1435@P46: native signers. BOBSL [22] supports large-scale British Sign Language recognition
L1436@P46: with approximately 1,467 hours of broadcast footage. CE-CNSL [31] is recommended
L1437@P46: for recognition under natural, complex environmental conditions, given its more than
L1438@P46-47: 70 distinct background settings.48

L1530@P48: 5.3.3 Sign Language Translation
L1531@P48: Sign language translation (SLT) requires sentence-level translations paired with
L1532@P48: signed video, a large vocabulary, and a sufficient signer pool. RWTH-PHOENIXWeather 2014 [23] is the most established SLT benchmark. For American Sign
L1533@P48: Language, How2Sign [18], YouTube-ASL [20], and OpenASL [21] are recommended;
L1534@P48: OpenASL [21] is the largest, with 288 hours and over 33,000 vocabulary items.
L1535@P48: CSL-Daily [32] supports Chinese Sign Language translation. Mediapi-RGB [38]
L1536@P48: is recommended for French Sign Language, providing 86 hours produced by deaf
L1537@P48: journalists. KSL-Guide [42] supports Korean Sign Language translation and includes
L1538@P48: a unique subset of interrogative sentences.
