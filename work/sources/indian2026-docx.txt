AI-Based Indian Sign Language Recognition: A Review of the SignFlow Framework, Results and Open Challenges

Abstract
Sign language recognition (SLR) translates visual gestures into text or speech and can narrow the communication gap between signers and non-signers. In India, about 18 million deaf people depend on Indian Sign Language (ISL), yet government, healthcare and digital services rarely support it. Recognising a whole sentence of signs (continuous SLR, or CSLR) is much harder than recognising single signs, and doing so in real time adds constraints of latency, device variation and signer variation. This paper reviews AI-based approaches to continuous ISL recognition and gives a detailed, critical study of SignFlow, a real-time CSLR network proposed by Geetha et al. (IEEE Access, 2025). SignFlow combines a mixed-convolution 3D ResNet (MC3_18) for spatial features with an 8-layer Transformer encoder for temporal modelling, trained end to end with Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC). Both components are pre-trained on ISL data, the CNN on isolated word videos and the Transformer on MediaPipe pose and hand features, and the input pipeline uses alignment correction and similarity-based down-sampling to 6 frames per second. We describe the datasets, architecture, training strategy, metrics (Word Error Rate and the Detection Rate), ablations and deployment results reported for SignFlow, including a WER of 19 on the Continuous ISL corpus and a load test of 74 concurrent users on one GPU. We then discuss strengths, weaknesses in the evidence, applications and future research directions. This is a review: all SignFlow results are those reported by its original authors and no new experiments are claimed.
Keywords: Indian Sign Language, continuous sign language recognition, SignFlow, 3D CNN, Transformer, CTC, MediaPipe, real-time recognition, accessibility
I. Introduction
Sign languages are complete natural languages that carry meaning through hand shape, hand movement, facial expression, head movement and body posture. They have their own vocabulary and grammar and are not word-for-word copies of spoken languages. For deaf communities they are tied to education, social life, culture and identity [1], [2]. Indian Sign Language (ISL) is used by an estimated 18 million deaf people in India. Although ISL is officially recognised, deaf citizens still face barriers in healthcare, education, legal services and welfare schemes, because few officials and service staff know ISL and because most websites, applications and announcements rely on spoken or written language [1].
Sign language recognition (SLR) is the area of artificial intelligence that converts sign gestures into text or speech. It is usually split into two problems [3], [4]:
Isolated SLR (ISLR): classify a short video of one sign into one word.
Continuous SLR (CSLR): read a longer video containing many consecutive signs and output the sequence of words (glosses) with no prior segmentation.
CSLR is harder because sign boundaries are unmarked, neighbouring signs blend into each other (co-articulation), and signers differ in speed, style, clothing and recording conditions. Frame-level labels are expensive to produce, so training normally relies on weak, sentence-level supervision.
Real-time CSLR is harder still. A deployed system must accept videos from phones and laptops with different frame rates and orientations, tolerate signers who are not centred in the frame, and answer quickly enough for a conversation. Many strong CSLR systems encode full RGB videos with heavy networks or fuse several cues, which gives good benchmark accuracy but makes web deployment difficult [1].
This paper makes the following contributions:
It surveys the main families of deep-learning CSLR methods and the datasets used to evaluate them.
It gives a structured account of SignFlow [1], the first reported real-time CSLR framework designed specifically for ISL, covering data engineering, pre-training, architecture, training, decoding and metrics.
It consolidates the reported results and ablations and critically examines what the evidence does and does not show.
It identifies applications, limitations and research directions for ISL recognition.

II. Background
A. Modules of a CSLR system
Deep-learning CSLR systems are commonly organised into three modules [1], [4]:
Feature extractor (visual module): encodes short-term spatial and motion detail. CNNs are the usual choice. 2D CNNs give a frame-wise embedding, whereas 3D CNNs process several frames together and give one embedding per overlapping clip, which captures motion directly.
Sequence learner: captures long-range context. Earlier systems used LSTM and BiLSTM networks; newer systems use the Transformer [22], whose self-attention models long-range dependencies.
Alignment module: links video clips to glosses during training. CTC is the standard solution when frame-level labels are absent.
B. Connectionist Temporal Classification
CTC adds a special blank symbol to the gloss vocabulary and lets the network emit one distribution over glosses and blank at every time step. The probability of a target gloss sequence is the sum over all frame-to-gloss alignments that collapse to it, where collapsing merges repeated predictions and removes blanks. For example, the frame predictions blood, blood, blank, donation, donation, blank, register, register, how, how collapse to blood donation register how. Training maximises p(Y|X) for the correct sequence. At inference the goal is
Y* = argmax over Y of p(Y | X),
which is approximated by greedy decoding or, more accurately, by beam search with an optional length penalty [26], [27].
C. Evaluation
CSLR is evaluated by the Word Error Rate (WER) [23]:
WER = (insertions + deletions + substitutions) / (number of words in the reference).
Lower is better. WER is order-sensitive and treats every word equally, which is not always what an application needs; Section VII discusses a complementary metric.
III. Related Work
A. Feature extraction and sequence learning
Zhu et al. [5] proposed MSTNet, which combines a multi-scale temporal block with Transformers to improve temporal feature extraction without prior knowledge. Hu et al. [6] proposed AdaSize, which cuts spatial redundancy by adjusting frame resolution dynamically through a recurrent policy network, lowering computation and memory while keeping state-of-the-art accuracy. Zuo and Mak [7] used feature disentanglement to remove signer-specific information from the backbone, giving a signer-independent recogniser.
B. State-of-the-art architectures
The strongest reported results come from ConSignformer [8], which combines a Conformer (a Transformer variant) with an S3D CNN and was designed with practical use in mind. TwoStream-SLT [9] reduced WER with multiple auxiliary losses and Spatial Pyramid Networks and reused the pre-training strategy of its SingleStream predecessor [10], but it lacks a Transformer backbone, which may hurt on long sequences. Multi-cue networks that fuse hands, face and body streams [11]–[14] improve accuracy but add complexity and sensitivity to noisy data. A more efficient direction is single-cue modelling with cross-modal alignment: CVT-SLR [15] uses a variational auto-encoder for sequence learning, and SMKD [16] uses a 2D CNN–BiLSTM–CTC network with a three-stage optimisation strategy.
C. Training strategies and alignment
Staged optimisation [17] and iterative training [18], [19] strengthen the feature extractor and reduce overfitting. The Visual Alignment Constraint of Min et al. [20] adds alignment supervision to the visual module. All of these rely on CTC to learn alignments. The authors of SignFlow also examined absolute and relative positional encodings for Transformers in sign recognition and translation in earlier work [21].
D. Research gap
The literature shows that (i) strong systems target German benchmark data, (ii) many are heavy or multi-cue and not meant for real-time web deployment, (iii) pre-training is mostly on general action-recognition data rather than sign data, and (iv) practical issues such as variable frame rates and off-centre signers are rarely handled. SignFlow was designed to address these gaps for ISL.

IV. Datasets
A. Benchmark datasets
The usual CSLR benchmarks are RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather 2014 [23] and PHOENIX-2014T [24]. Both are German, recorded in a controlled studio with a plain background and a fixed camera. They allow fair algorithm comparison but differ from real use, where backgrounds, clothing, devices and orientations vary.
B. The UMANG-eRaktkosh ISL corpora
No public ISL dataset for CSLR existed, so the SignFlow authors collected two corpora [1]. The continuous corpus holds sentence-level videos based on frequently asked questions (FAQs) of the eRaktkosh blood-bank service on the UMANG application, with gloss-level annotation. The isolated corpus holds videos of single words from the service vocabulary and is used for pre-training. The authors state that the dataset will be published in the near future; at the time of the paper it was not yet public.

Recordings were made by certified ISL interpreters approved by the Rehabilitation Council of India, assisted by deaf students under the interpreters' guidance. Signs follow the standards of the Indian Sign Language Research and Training Centre (ISLRTC). Where no standard sign existed for a word, widely used regional variants were included, and all valid variants of a word were kept.
V. The SignFlow Framework
A. Overview
SignFlow is a modular pipeline: a video is aligned, resized and down-sampled; a pre-trained 3D CNN converts it to clip embeddings; a pre-trained 8-layer Transformer encoder models the sequence; a softmax and CTC beam-search decoder produce a gloss sequence; and the chatbot uses the glosses to retrieve an answer. The design is intentionally simple compared with state-of-the-art models in order to give a lightweight network that is easy to deploy on the web [1].
Fig. 1 (to be drawn by the authors): block diagram: Signer video (Firebase) → Alignment correction → Resize 112×112 + similarity-based down-sampling to 6 fps → MC3_18 → 8-layer Transformer → Softmax + CTC beam search → Gloss sequence → Chatbot.
B. Data engineering and alignment correction
In a chatbot the video window is small and the signer may be off-centre, so a hand can leave the frame and part of the gesture is lost. SignFlow therefore applies an alignment-correction step [1]:
MediaPipe Pose locates the two ear landmarks; the midpoint of the line joining them is the signer's centre and the ear distance gives the scale.
The frame centre is computed from the image dimensions.
The x-shift and y-shift between the two centres are calculated.
The frame is shifted to bring the signer to the desired centre while preserving the aspect ratio.
For training, videos are resized and cropped to a 4:3 aspect ratio at 720 × 960 pixels. During live recognition, the aligned video is resized directly to 112 × 112 pixels, the input size of the model.
C. Dynamic similarity-based down-sampling
Devices record at different frame rates and processing every frame is slow, so every video is reduced to a common rate. During training, videos are down-sampled at random sampling rates, typically in the range 5 to 8. The video is divided into segments according to the actual and required frame rates and one frame is chosen at random from each segment, which yields many different views of the same video and increases training diversity. During inference, a random frame is not used; instead the frame most similar to the others in its segment is selected using a similarity metric, so that the model sees the most representative frames. The authors report that 6 fps gives a good balance of accuracy and speed. If an input video is below 6 fps it is treated as low quality, no prediction is made, and the user is asked to record again from another device or network.
D. CNN feature extractor and its pre-training
The visual backbone is MC3_18 (mixed-convolution 3D ResNet-18) from torchvision, already pre-trained on a general action-recognition dataset. Its first layers use 3D convolutions to capture motion and later layers use cheaper 2D convolutions, which keeps the network lighter than a full 3D ResNet. The authors argue that pre-training and fine-tuning data should be similar. They therefore further pre-train MC3_18 on the isolated ISL corpus as a video-classification (ISLR) task over the 163 vocabulary words, save the checkpoint, and fine-tune it during end-to-end CSLR training. In the final network, 2D pooling is applied to the last layers so that each overlapping clip is represented by one compact feature vector.
E. Transformer encoder and pose-based pre-training
The sequence learner is an 8-layer Transformer encoder with model dimension 512 and 8 attention heads, with residual addition and normalisation after each sub-layer. It is pre-trained on pose information from the Continuous ISL corpus. MediaPipe Pose and Hand estimates give X, Y and Z (depth) for each joint; only upper-body landmarks are used. Four groups of features are generated per frame [1]:
raw joint estimates;
bone lengths (Euclidean distances between connected joints);
relative positions of each joint with respect to adjacent or connected joints;
joint Euclidean distances (for example left palm to right palm, left palm to left shoulder, right palm to right shoulder).
Together these form a 443-dimensional vector per frame (hands plus pose), mapped to 512 dimensions by a linear layer. Frames in which MediaPipe fails to detect the hands leave gaps in the coordinate curves; these are filled by degree-2 polynomial interpolation. The pre-trained checkpoint initialises the Transformer of the final model.
F. End-to-end training with CTC
Because frame-level annotations are unavailable, SignFlow is fine-tuned end to end with CTC loss on sentence-level glosses. The softmax of the encoder output gives frame-level gloss probabilities and CTC marginalises over alignments. The authors trained two variants of the final model, with and without positional encoding in the Transformer. Using a Transformer with no position embedding is presented as a first for this task.
VI. Training and Implementation Details
A. Data augmentation
Augmentation enlarges the effective training set and improves generalisation. As reported in [1], training videos are resized to 112 × 112 pixels, randomly down-sampled at rates of 5 to 8, and subjected to random brightness, contrast, hue and saturation changes (to simulate lighting), random rotation of −4 to 4 degrees (small camera changes), coarse dropout (occlusion), random sun flare (extreme light), pixel dropout (to avoid overfitting to pixel patterns) and horizontal flipping (independence from hand dominance). Videos are collated and padded to a maximum length of 250 frames for batch processing.

VII. Evaluation and Results
A. Metrics: WER and Detection Rate
Besides WER, the authors introduce the Detection Rate:
Detection Rate = (words correctly recognised) / (words in reference),
counted without regard to word order. It suits the chatbot setting, where an answer is retrieved from the detected keywords and the correct answer can still be returned if the order is slightly wrong. For this reason the deployed model was chosen for the highest detection rate rather than the lowest WER [1].
B. Real-time test protocol
Testing was done in real time through the chatbot. Two sets of ISL gloss sequences were prepared: Test set 1, with all glosses inside the vocabulary, and Test set 2, which also contains out-of-vocabulary (OOV) glosses. Four models were compared: (a) CNN–Transformer with position encoding (PE); (b) CNN–Transformer without PE; (c) CNN with a pose-pre-trained Transformer with PE; (d) CNN with a pose-pre-trained Transformer without PE.
C. Results
Models with a pre-trained Transformer gave the lowest overall WER on both test sets, although their deletion rate rose noticeably; this was offset by lower insertion and substitution errors. For the chatbot, model (b), without pre-training and without PE, had the best detection rate on both test sets and was integrated into the application. The best WER reported on the Continuous ISL dataset is 19, obtained by the pose-pre-trained model with position encoding. The authors report that new signers, unseen in training, signing sequences that were present in the trained gloss set were recognised with 100 percent accuracy, and that a "best-case" test set (all words in vocabulary, detection rate above 50 percent) gave about 95 percent accuracy, which they take to support a signer-independent system. Typical errors are small insertions (an extra word) or deletions of short signs; with OOV glosses the model tends to substitute a similar in-vocabulary word or drop the word. The method also showed competitive performance on the German Phoenix-2014 and Phoenix-2014T datasets, previously published in [21].
D. Deployment and load test
In a 30-minute load test with users on different devices and networks, a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU with 40 GB memory served a peak of 74 concurrent users. This supports the claim that the lightweight design is suitable for web deployment.

IX. Critical Discussion
SignFlow is a well-motivated, deployment-oriented system, and several of its ideas are practical contributions. This section separates what the evidence supports from what remains uncertain. The points below are our own reading of [1].
A. Strengths
Domain-matched pre-training. Pre-training the CNN on isolated ISL words and the Transformer on pose features exploits the similarity between pre-training and target data and gives clear WER gains in the ablation (for example 43 to 19 with PE).
Practical pre-processing. Alignment correction and similarity-based down-sampling to 6 fps address real deployment problems (off-centre signers, mixed devices) that benchmark-oriented work seldom handles.
Application-aware evaluation. The Detection Rate recognises that, for retrieval-based chatbots, finding the keywords matters more than word order, and the deployed model was selected on that basis.
Lightweight design. A single 3D ResNet plus an 8-layer encoder, with a reported 74 concurrent users on one GPU, is attractive for low-cost public services.
A needed resource. The two corpora, with 13 signers, certified interpreters and ISLRTC-standard signs, fill a real gap if released.
B. Points that need caution
Small, scripted, in-domain evaluation. The vocabulary is 163 words and 226 sentences from one service domain, and the real-time test sets were authored by the researchers. WER 19 is therefore not comparable with results on open-vocabulary benchmarks such as PHOENIX-2014T.
WER versus deployment choice. The lowest-WER model (19, pre-trained with PE) is not the one deployed; the deployed model (b) has a much higher WER (about 0.25 to 0.27 from the charts). Readers should not read WER 19 as the accuracy of the live system. Model (b) also has the highest detection rate, yet the detection rate itself is only about 0.63 on in-vocabulary sentences and 0.42 with OOV words, so a large share of reference words is still missed.
Evidence for signer independence. The 100 percent and 95 percent figures are described for particular favourable test conditions (known gloss sequences, in-vocabulary words, detection rate above 50 percent). Since 13 signers are used for training and the number and demographics of unseen test signers are not detailed, a stronger protocol (a signer-disjoint split reported with confidence intervals) would be needed to establish signer independence.
Approximate numbers. Several results appear only as charts, so exact values and variance across runs are not available.
Ambiguity in the pre-training description. The text describes the Transformer as pre-trained with CTC on continuous videos, but also states that both networks were pre-trained on isolated word videos for a classification task, and the pose network diagram ends in an ISL word output. A reproduction would need this clarified.
Non-manual signals. Facial expression and head movement, which carry grammatical information in ISL, are not explicitly modelled, and only upper-body pose and hands are used.
Data availability. Until the datasets are published, independent verification is not possible.
X. Applications
Government and public services: sign-enabled chatbots, such as the eRaktkosh assistant on the UMANG application, give deaf citizens access to services without an interpreter.
Healthcare: deaf patients can ask about appointments, blood availability or procedures at kiosks or help desks.
Education: classroom interaction, ISL-learning tools and e-learning content for deaf students.
Customer support: banks, railways, telecom and retail help-desks offering video support in ISL.
Legal and emergency assistance: communication with police, courts and emergency services where interpreters are scarce.
Research: the corpora, pose-based pre-training and detection-rate metric can be reused for other Indian domains and for sign language translation.
XI. Limitations and Future Directions
A. Limitations reported for SignFlow
Out-of-vocabulary signs make accurate recognition difficult and also disturb the detection of in-vocabulary words. Fast signing raises deletion errors because of co-articulation. The vocabulary is restricted to the eRaktkosh domain, so general ISL conversation is unsupported. Portrait recordings often cut off the hands, at least 6 fps is needed for prediction, and multiple people in the frame reduce accuracy.
B. Research directions
Larger and more diverse data: more words, sentences and signers, including fast signers, to reduce OOV and deletion errors.
OOV handling: detect unknown signs and ask the user to rephrase, or learn new signs from few examples.
Attention-based sequence-to-sequence models: explore alternatives to CTC that may improve alignment, as suggested in [1].
Keyword-oriented optimisation: train and select models directly for detection rate.
Non-manual cues: add facial expression and head movement features.
Sign language translation: convert glosses to fluent text or speech in English and Indian languages, and generate ISL output for two-way communication.
Edge deployment: compress the model for smartphones to lower server cost and latency.
Robust evaluation: signer-disjoint splits, open-vocabulary test sets and public benchmarks for ISL.
XII. Conclusion
This paper reviewed AI-based continuous Indian Sign Language recognition and examined SignFlow [1], a real-time framework that combines a 3D CNN pre-trained on isolated ISL words with an 8-layer Transformer pre-trained on MediaPipe pose and hand features, trained end to end with CTC. According to its authors, ISL-specific pre-training, alignment correction and similarity-based down-sampling to 6 fps make real-time recognition practical on varied devices, with a WER of 19 on the Continuous ISL dataset and 74 concurrent users served by one GPU. The Detection Rate metric shows that the best model for an application depends on the task and not only on WER, and the finding that a Transformer can work well without position encoding for constrained, word-level tasks is noteworthy. At the same time, the evidence comes from a small, single-domain, partly chart-reported evaluation, and OOV signs, fast signing and the lack of non-manual features limit general use. Larger public datasets, rigorous signer-independent protocols and open-vocabulary modelling are the main steps toward making digital services accessible to India's deaf community.
