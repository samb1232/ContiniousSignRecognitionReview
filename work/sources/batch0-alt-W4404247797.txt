Abstract: Accessibility in online platforms is a critica l concern in our increasingly digital world , 
where information and services are predominantly accessed through the Internet. The purpose of 
this systematic review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the current state of the art in online 
accessibility technologies, and it is focused on key tools such as sign language recognition, speech -
to-text, text-to-speech, and voice recognition. Despite advancements in digital inclusivity, numerous 
technical limitations persist, which limit the accessibility of online content for individuals with dis-
abilities. Our findings indicate that while speech and voice technologies have achieved good accu-
racies and low word error rates, further research is needed to improve the accuracy and usability of 
sign language recogn ition systems, especially for continuous sign language recognition, as they 
have low accuracy. In this review, we analyzed research articles and publications from well-known 
databases, including Google Scholar, Elsevier, IEEE Xplore, and Springer. In order to ensure a high 
standard of quality, we applied the PRISMA 2020 and PEDro methodologies to quantitatively and 
qualitatively filter the thousands of articles provided by these databases, and we selected only stud-
ies that were related to our study. Key areas of investigation included the performance and accuracy 
of sign language interfaces, speech-to-text, text-to-speech, and speech recognition applications and 
the compatibility of these technologies with different platforms and devices. Th is review also ex-
plores the role of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning 
(ML) in enhancing accessibility and personalizing user experiences. Through a critical analysis of 
current solutions and a discussion of existing gaps, this paper offers insights into potential improve-
ments and future directions for creating more accessible online environments. The findings might 
be valuable to researchers and developers dedicated to promoting digital inclusivity and equality. 
Keywords: sign language recognition; accessibility and interfaces; machine learning; artificial  
intelligence; text-to-speech; speech-to-text; voice recognition 
 
1. Introduction 
Currently, there are 1.3 billion people with different types of disabilities [1] who face 
significant challenges when it comes to using online platforms. From an accessibility point 
of view, many of these individuals encounter a multitude of barriers that limit their ability 
to access and interact with web content effectively. In so me cases, the lack of proper  ac-
cessibility features can make certain platforms completely unusable for individuals with  
specific needs, so they are excluded from the benefits and opportunities offered by the  
digital world. This situation not only impacts t he personal and social lives of those af-
fected but also limits their ability to participate fully in education, employment, and other 
essential activities. 
In order to address these issues, there are internationally recognized standards and  
guidelines that web developers and designers are expected to follow when creating online 
platforms. One of the most recognized and largely adopted sets of guidelines is known as

the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1. These guidelines are developed by 
the World Wide Web Consortium, and they provide a comprehensive framework aimed 
at making web content more accessible to a broader range of users, including individuals 
with disabilities. The WCAG 2.1 revolve around various principles and criteria designed 
to accommodate people with diverse needs such as blindness and low vision, deafness 
and hearing loss, limited mobility, speech disabilities, cognitive and neurological impair-
ments, and others. By adhering to these guidelines, d evelopers can create websites that 
are perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust , thus promoting a more inclusive 
digital environment [2]. 
Despite the existence of these standards, implementing effective accessibility solu-
tions often requires the i ntegration of advanced technologies such as ML algorithms. 
These algorithms play a crucial role in developing interfaces designed for specific disabil-
ities by providing functionalities like text-to-speech (TTS) and speech recognition (SR) for 
those with visual impairments, speech-to-text (STT) for individuals with hearing loss, and 
sign language recognition (SLR) for those with speech or auditory disabilities. Addition-
ally, other assistive technologies may include alternative navigation methods for those 
with limited mobility, predictive text input, and automated captioning for video content. 
The application of ML and AI in these areas has the potential to significantly enhance user 
experience (UX) by creating more intuitive, responsive , and personalized interactions for 
users with diverse accessibility needs. 
While there has been considerable progress in accessibility tools like STT, TTS, and  
voice recognition, several critical gaps remain, particularly in the area of SLR. Sign lan-
guage recognition still lacks the precision necessary for consistent usability, especially in  
continuous video contexts [3]. As a result, accessibility solutions remain inconsistent, with 
many platforms still unable to accommodate users with specific needs fully.  
Through this systematic review, we aim to thoroughly explore and analyze the cur-
rent state of the art in developing accessible online interfaces that incorporate ML algo-
rithms, particularly analyzing the accuracy, precision, and word error rates of these algo-
rithms. By examining the latest research and advancements in the field, we seek to identify 
gaps and limitations in existing technologies and methodologies. Our goal is to highlight 
areas where improvements are necessary to achieve better algorithm accuracy, enhance 
UX, or make previously inaccessible platforms more usable.  
This review will provide valuable insights for researchers, developers, and stake-
holders involved in the creation of inclusive digital environments, guiding future efforts 
to optimize accessibility and promote digital equity.  
Our research paper aims to answer the following questions:  
• What are the similarity scores for speech-to-text algorithms and word-level intelligi-
bility of text-to-speech algorithms?  
• What are the word error rates and recognition rates of speech recognition algorithms?  
• Are there sign language recognition algorithms that have an accuracy higher than 
95% for continuous SLR?  
• What are the methods used to build sign language recognition algorithms?  
In the next chapter, we  will describe the methodology we used to filter the selected  
articles that contain valuable information such as accuracy/precision of ML algorithms  
that could be used to implement accessible interfaces (STT, TTS, SR , and SLR) for people 
suffering from speech impairments, deafness, and blindness.  
2. Research Methodology 
This section outlines the protocol used to identify, gather , and evaluate the state-of-
the-art techniques examined in this systematic review. The process is divided into four 
key phases: development of the research strategy, formulation of the research questions, 
criteria for article selection, and presentation of the next steps in our research.

2.1. Developing the Research Strategy 
In our study , we put together a solid collection of research papers on our topic to 
obtain a well-rounded view and an in-depth understanding of the latest progress and ad-
vancements in accessibility technologies. The articles we collected were published be-
tween 2018 and 2024, representing the most recent developments in this field. To ensure 
a wider scope, we used papers from multiple reputable databases such as Google Scholar, 
IEEE Xplore, Elsevier , and Springer. Our search strategy involved using various combi-
nations of keywords and key phrases, such as “Sign Language Recognition”, “Developing 
Accessible Interfaces”,  “Speech Recognition”, “Speech to Text Interfaces” , and “Text to 
Speech Interfaces”. This extensive search initially returned 2819 articles.  
We applied the PRISMA and PEDro methodologies to rigorously filter the large vol-
ume of studies identified in our initial search. PRISMA provided a structured approach for 
organizing and documenting the selection process. By detailing each step of the study se-
lection process, PRISMA allowed us to systematically narrow down the results to the studies 
that were most relevant to our focus on online accessibility technologies. Meanwhile, the 
PEDro scale was essential for assessing the methodological quality of these studies.  
Given the large number of articles received, we implemented a multi -stage research 
strategy that involved both quantitative and qualitative filtering to identify the most rele-
vant and high-quality studies. One of the first challenges we encountered in our  filtering 
process was the large number of duplicate articles. In order to solve this, we had to aggre-
gate all the results from the searches through an Excel file and use Microsoft Excel features 
to identify the duplicate results and remove them. After thi s step, the number of  articles 
was reduced by 446 articles, leaving us with 2373 unique papers.  
Following this, we applied a quantitative filter based on specific paper characteristics. 
We required each article to have been cited at least ten times, indic ating a certain level of 
recognition and impact within the community. After applying these criteria, we reduced  
the pool to 2285 articles.  
We conducted a more targeted filtering process by reviewing the titles and abstracts  
of the remaining articles. During this phase, we filtered the title and abstract with addi-
tional keywords, including “ Accessible Interface”, “Speech to text”, “Text to speech”, “ac-
cessibility”, “Speech recognition”, and “sign language recognition”. This step allowed us 
to exclude 2176 articles that did not align closely with our main research focus. After  this 
extensive screening, 109 articles appeared directly relevant to our study.  
From these 109 articles, we applied a qualitative filtering based on a set of questions  
that will be described in the following subsection. After this step, 35 articles remained.

2.2. Formulation of the Research Questions 
After completing the quantitative filtering using PRISMA filtering, we conducted  
qualitative filtering using the PEDro methodology to obtain more relevant results related 
to our main research topic. The qualitative filtering involved asking a set of questions that 
each article should address. If a research paper answered at least two questions from the  
filtering process, we included it. The questions for qualitative filtering were the following:  
1. What are the methods used to implement an accessible interface?  
2. Is it using voice recognition, text-to-speech, speech-to-text, or sign language recogni-
tion algorithms? 
3. Is the research aiming to help the vulnerable categories of people with disabilities?  
4. Is the machine learning algorithm producing good results and does it have good ac-
curacy? 
5. Is the proposed solution implemented, adapted, or proposed to a real-life use case?  
As we have mentioned before, after the qualitative filtering, we removed 74 articles  
from the 109 initially selected, leaving 35 remaining articles.  
2.3. Criteria for Article Selection 
As a conclusion based on what we said in the previous subsections, the criteria for  
article selection were the following:

1. Quantitative filtering (PRISMA filtering):  
(a) Articles published between 2018–2024;  
(b) Articles that were written only in the English language;

It is important to mention that firstly, we applied quantitative filtering to narrow

filtering, we read the abstract of the 109 remain ing papers, and we searched for specific

nition. We removed the articles that were not aligned or related to our study (e.g. , acces-
sibility not related to machine learning or artificial intelligence).

After conducting searches across the scientific databases and identifying relevant re-

most effective approaches, and highlight potential areas for future exploration.

3.1. Relevant Solutions Based on Speech Recognition 
Voice recognition technology powers secure user authentication and personalized  
experiences. Applications include voice -based biometrics in banking (e.g. , Wells Fargo’s 
voice authentication [6]), smart home devices (e.g., Google Nest), and call centers for cus-
tomer identity verification. Challenges in voice recognition include handling variations in 
voice due to health, age, and emotional state, which can lead to inaccuracies and  frustra-
tions in sensitive contexts like financial services.  
Advancements in ASR technologies have shown varied accuracy rates among leading 
solutions, with notable performances from providers like Whisper and Speechmatics [7]. 
These findings highlight the importance of evaluating ASR systems not only for their accu-
racy but also for their accessibility features. Research indicates that voice -controlled per-
sonal assistants, such as the Amazon Echo, are beneficial for users with disabilities, particu-
larly for those with speech impairments [8]. The positive user feedback underscores the po-
tential of these technologies to enhance communication and interaction in daily life.  
Subhash, S. et al. discuss in their work the growing role of voice control technology, 
particularly AI -based voice assistants used in smartphones and laptops [9]. These

assistants recognize human voice, convert it to text , and then use the Google text -to-

(both 2.7 and 3.x versions)  play sound package. Also, the study of Pradhan, A. et al. ex-

Another interesting study conducted by Branham, S. M. et al . says that voice -acti-
vated personal assistants (VAPAs), like Siri and Alexa, benefit users, especially those with 
visual impairments [10]. This study reviews design guidelines from major companies, 
highlighting the assumption that VAPAs should mimic human conversation. It critiques 
this approach and suggests improvements to enhance usability for all users. On the other 
hand, Blue, L.  et al. demonstrates that the Two Microphone Authentication system can 
accurately localize audio sources within a narrow cone of less than 30 ° while achieving 
zero false positives [11]. It effectively eliminates replay attacks and prevents the injection 
of inaudible or hidden commands, significantly enhancing the security of voice-controlled 
interfaces. Canonico, M. et al. review and compare various cloud-based natural language 
understanding platforms developed over the last decade. It presents a taxonomy for six 
platforms and conducts a performance evaluation of three of them, highlighting their re-
spective strengths and weaknesses. The analysis reflects the widespread adoption of these 
tools in chat services and conversational assistants on mobile devices [12]. Darabkh, K. A. 
et al. present an SR system designed to help arm -disabled students to control computers 
by voice, enhancing accessibility in education. The system uses two approaches: one based 
on voice activity detection and Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients and another using dis-
crete wavelet transform with modified MFCC. The first approach achieved a recognition 
rate of 98.7%, while the second achieved a rate of 98.86%, offering higher accuracy but 
slower processing [13]. In order to explore recent advancements in accessibility research, 
Brewer, R. N. et al . situates voice -based accessibility within existing theoretical frame-
works, discusses design challenges, and identifies opportunities for interdisciplinary col-
laboration in this area [14]. In the same context, Kuhn, K. et al. highlight the measuring of 
accuracy of ASR solutions. The word error rates were as follows: 22.4% for Google, 12.4% 
for IBM, 10.8% for Deepgram, 10.5% for SpeechText.AI, 5.6% for Rev AI, 5.3% for Amazon 
and Microsoft, 4.7% for Tencent,  4.3% for AssemblyAI, 3.4% for Speechmatics , and 2.9% 
for Whisper [7].  
The advancements in SR algorithms have reached a mature level, demonstrating sig-
nificant improvements in accuracy and functionality across various applications. Leading 
solutions have achieved impressively low word error rates, indicating a high degree of  
reliability in real-world usage.

3.2. Solutions Based on Speech-to-Text and Text-to-Speech 
Speech-to-text technology has seen significant advancements in accuracy, largely due 
to improvements in natural language processing and deep learning models. It  is widely 
used in applications like transcription services (e.g., Otter.ai and Google Live Transcribe), 
virtual assistants (e.g., Siri, Alexa), and closed captioning for accessibility in media. Real -
world challenges include handling diverse accents, noisy environments, and domain-spe-
cific terminology, which can affect user satisfaction and the overall effectiveness of STT.  
Text-to-speech systems have advanced to generate more natural and expressive voices. 
These systems are now integral to assistive technologies like screen readers for visually im-
paired users (e.g., JAWS and NVDA) and language learning apps (e.g., Duolingo). TTS are 
integrated in many real-world applications such as GPS systems that provide spoken direc-
tions, virtual assistants (e.g., Siri, Google Assistant, Alexa), and accessibility tools for indi-
viduals with visual impairments (e.g., screen readers like JAWS and VoiceOver).  
Recent advancements in TTS technology have led to remarkable accuracy and per-
formance levels, particularly in models like Tacotron 2, which achieved a n MOS of 4.38 , 
and Tacotron 1, which produced a n MOS of 4.01 [15]. Additionally, an almost unsuper-
vised learning method for TTS aimed at low -resource languages demonstrated excep-
tional results, with a 99.84% word-level intelligibility but a 2.68 MOS on the LJSpeech da-
taset, despite utilizing only 200 paired data points and additional unpaired data [16].  
Zen, H. et al . present “LibriTTS”, a speech corpus designed for TTS applications, 
based on the LibriSpeech dataset. It includes 585 h of 24 kHz speech data from 2456 speak-
ers. Models trained on LibriTTS achieved mean opinion scores (MOS) above 4.0 for natu-
ralness in five out of six evaluation speakers [17]. Trivedi, A. et al . explore applications 
that facilita te text and speech communication, including SR, STT conversion, synthetic 
speech generation, and language translation. The focus is on the methods employed to 
ensure seamless  interaction between parties across networks [18]. In the same context, 
Khan, A. et al. introduce a blind-friendly user interface framework that simplifies interface 
components based on user profiles and context. An empirical study with 41 participants 
demonstrated significant improvements in their smartphone interaction experience [19].  
Anandan, M. et al . present an object detection and navigation system for visually 
impaired individuals, featuring static and dynamic obstacle detection. The static module 
uses techniques like SURF feature extraction, while the dynamic module employs

Gaussian mixture models [20]. Visual information is converted into audio feedback via a 
Raspberry Pi, including vibrations for users with partial hearing loss. The system effec-
tively models backgrounds, eliminates shadows , and supports blind users in navigati ng 
their environment.  
Aguilar-Chacon, J. E. et al. explore the integration of small-sized computers with STT 
technology, particularly addressing the challenge of implementing STT algorithms on mi-
crocontroller-based systems without compromising performance . A methodology is pre-
sented to evaluate STT services using similarity and speed as metrics, with an experiment 
conducted on a Raspberry Pi 3 for Spanish speakers. The results reveal that Google’s STT  
system outperformed both IBM and Microsoft, showing fas ter conversion times and 
higher accuracy, with a similarity score of 97.116%, compared to Microsoft’s 90.825% and 
IBM’s 90.337% [21].  
Following the experiments of other researchers in this field, Khanam, F. et al. created 
a literature review where they discuss the process of TTS synthesis, which converts natu-
ral-language text into speech using recorded samples stored in a database. The review 
highlights the significant advancements in TTS due to deep learning, compared to tradi-
tional machine  learning methods. The paper introduces a taxonomy of popular deep 
learning architectures and models used in speech synthesis, reviews various datasets uti-
lized in TTS, and describes common evaluation metrics for assessing synthesized speech 
quality [15]. It concludes by outlining the challenges and future directions for TTS systems. 
Ren, Y. et al. present an almost unsupervised learning method for TTS and automatic speech 
recognition (ASR) aimed at low -resource languages, addressing the challenge of limited 
aligned data. The approach combines several components: a denoising auto-encoder for lan-
guage modeling; dual transformation to enhance accuracy between TTS and ASR; bidirec-
tional sequence modeling to reduce error propagation; and a unified transformer-based 
model structure. The method achieves 99.84% word-level intelligibility, a 2.68 MOS for TTS, 
and an 11.7% phoneme error rate for ASR on the LJSpeech dataset, using only 200 paired 
data points (about 20 min of audio) and additional unpaired data [16].

interest in the earlier years, followed by an increase in interest around 2021. This similarity 
suggests a correlated rise in the relevance of both technologies, possibly due to the increasing 
adoption of voice-enabled applications, digital assistants, and accessibility solutions.  
The simultaneous increase in search interest might indicate that users are increas-
ingly seeking or using both “speech to text” and “text to speech” functionalities, poten-
tially driven by advancements in AI, improvements in accessibility technologies, and the 
rise of remote  work and online co mmunication. This parallel growth could reflect a 
broader trend toward integrating voice-based technologies in online platforms and other 
digital environments.

3.3. Advanced Solutions on Sign Language Recognition 
SLR is a newer and less mature field compared to other recognition technologies, 
often limited by expensive datasets and language variations [22]. Current applications are 
mainly in experimental settings, but some real-world implementations are emerging, like 
translating sign language for customer service desks (e.g., SignAll’s software). Challenges 
include capturing nuanced hand and facial expressions, high computational require-
ments, and adapting to different sign languages, which limit SLR’s broader adoption and 
user satisfaction.  
Recent advancements in SLR have demonstrated impressive accuracy levels across  
various models and approaches. For instance, a model trained on 54,049 images of Arabic 
sign language achieved a precision rate of 95% [23]. In another study, the SAM-SLR frame-
work integrated multiple modalities and reported recognition rates of 98.42% for  RGB 
images and 98.53% for RGBD [24]. Additionally, a hierarchical model for ISL gestures  
reached 98.52% accuracy for one -hand gestures [25], while a wearable SLR devi ce using 
electromyography and inertial sensors achieved an average accuracy of 96.13% [26]. These 
achievements highlight the potential of advanced techniques in enhancing the reliability 
of SLR systems.  
Zakariah, M. et al . trained a model using 54 ,049 images with Arabic sign language. 
The model had a precision rate of 95% [23]. Also, Rastgoo, R. et al . conducted a survey  
that reviews recent vision -based sign language recognition models utilizing deep learn-
ing, highlighting accuracy improvements and ongoing challenges. It categorizes models 
for isolated and continuous recognition, discussing applications, datasets, hybrid ap-
proaches, and future research directions [27]. Jiang, S. et al. propose a novel multi-modal 
framework, SAM-SLR, for SLR that integrates skeleton-based, RGB, and depth modalities 
to enhance recognition accuracy. The model achieved a good performance, with recogni-
tion rates of 98.42% for RGB and 98.53% for RGBD, making it the top performer in the 
2021 SLR Challenge [24].  
Wen, F. et al. developed an AI-enabled sign language recognition system combining  
sensing gloves, deep learning , and a virtual reality interface. Their model recognize d 50 
words and 20 sentences, achieving an 86.67% accuracy for new sentence constructions by  
recombining word elements [28]. Camgoz, N. C. et al. present a transformer-based model 
that jointly learns sign language recognition and translation, achieving state-of-the-art re-
sults on the PHOENIX14T dataset. Their approach significantly improves translation per-
formance, with BLEU -4 scores rising from 9.58 to 21.80 , and sets new baselines for  sign 
language translation tasks [29]. Wadhawan, A. et al . present a deep -learning-based con-
volutional neural network (CNN) model for Indian sign language (ISL) recognition, using 
35,000 images of 100 static signs. The system, evaluated on 50 CNN models, achieves  a 
top training accuracy of 99.72% for colored images and 99.90% for grayscale images [30]. 
Li, D. et al. introduce the  large-scale Word-Level American Sign Language (ASL) dataset, 
featuring over 2000 words performed by more than 100 signers, the largest public ASL  
dataset available. The authors evaluate two deep learning models for word -level sign  
recognition: a holistic visual appearance-based approach and a 2D human pose-based ap-
proach, achieving a top-10 result of up to 62.63% accuracy [31].

A robust analysis of gesture recognition techniques was performed by Sharma, A. et 
al. for ISL using three models: fine-tuned VGG16, transfer learning VGG16 , and a hierar-
chical neural network. The hierarchical model achieved the best accuracy, with 98.52% for 
one-hand gestures and 97% for two -hand gestures [25]. Khomami, S. A. et al . present a 
wearable low-cost SLR device using surface electromyography and inertial measurement 
unit sensors to enhance accuracy. Data from ten volunteers performing 20 Persian sign 
language signs were collected, with 25 key features extracted and classified using a KNN 
classifier, achieving an average accuracy of 96.13% [26]. In the same context, Kunjumon, 
J. et al. propose a system that recognizes ISL and converts it to speech and text in English 
and Malayalam, displaying the output on an Android device, with an accuracy of approx-
imately 90% [32].  
Rao, G. A. et al. address the challenge of recognizing ISL gestures using convolutional 
neural networks. A dataset was created featuring five subjects performing 200 signs from 
different angles and backgrounds, with each sign captured in 60 frames. The study in-
volved training CNNs with various s ample sizes and architectures, achieving a recogni-
tion rate  of 92.88%, outperforming other classifiers on the same dataset. The proposed 
method enables independent use of a sign language recognition mobile application by 
hearing impaired individuals [33]. Huang, J. et al. propose LS-HAN, a framework for con-
tinuous sign language recognition that eliminates temporal segmentation. It uses a two -
stream CNN and hierarchical attention network, achieving up to 82.7% accuracy [34].  
Pu, J. et al . introduce an alignment network with iterative optimization for weakly  
supervised continuous sign language recognition. It combines a 3D  ResNet for feature  
learning and an encoder –decoder network with CTC for sequence modeling, optimized  
iteratively. Evaluated on large datasets, the method achieved a precision of 93.9% [35]. 
Mittal, A. et al. propose a modified LSTM model for continuous SLR, capable of recogniz-
ing sequences of connected gestures by splitting them into sub -units. Tested on 942 ISL  
sentences using 35 sign words, the system achieved an average accuracy of 72.3% for  
signed sentences and 89.5% for isolated words [3]. Bragg, D. et al. emphasize the need for 
interdisciplinary collaboration to develop sign language recognition, generation , and 
translation systems. It summarizes a workshop with experts from various fields, review-
ing the state of the art, identifying key challenges, and calling for more integrated research 
efforts in the community [22]. Murali, R. S. L. et al . introduce a system for recognizing  
ASL gestures using computer vision techniques. It captures hand gestures via a webcam, 
processes the images using the HSV color algorithm , and uses a CNN for classification. 
The model successfully recognizes 10 ASL alphabets with above 90% accuracy [36]. Kasap-
başi, A. et al . developed an ASL recognition dataset and a CNN model to interpret ASL 
gestures into natural language. The dataset accounts for various conditions, marking a 
significant advancement in sign language re cognition. The CNN achieved 99.38% accu-
racy with a low loss of 0.0250, outperforming two existing datasets. These results suggest 
that the system could be a promising solution for medical applications using deep learn-
ing while enhancing contributions to the SLR field [37].  
Bantupalli, K. et al . created a vision-based application that translates sign language  
into text, enhancing communication between signers and non -signers. It processes video  
sequences to extract features, using an inception CNN for spatial recognition and an RNN 
for temporal training, based on the ASL dataset. The accuracy using  a Softmax layer was 
around 90% [38]. 
However, continuous sign language recognition from videos presents more signifi-
cant challenges, often resulting in lower accu racy rates. While static recognition benefits 
from clear and defined gestures, dynamic recognition must contend with variations in 
speed, context, and environmental factors, which can limit performance. For instance, de-
spite advancements, the overall effectiveness of recognizing continuous signing from vid-
eos has not reached the same levels of accuracy seen with individual images. Addressing 
these challenges will be crucial for improving real -time, continuous sign language recog-
nition systems.

3.4. Other Relevant Studies 
There were also articles that highlighted the potential for AI technologies to signifi-
cantly impact people with disabilities (PWD), especially through tools like automated  SR 
and language prediction systems. While these AI systems can enhance accessibility,  they 
can also risk marginalizing PWD if not designed and tested carefully. The paper calls  at-
tention to fairness in AI for PWD, identifying concerns about how AI systems may fail or  
discriminate if care is not taken in development [39].  
In other articles, authors discuss the impact of artificial intelligence, particularly ma-
chine learning, on fair treatment for p eople with disabilities. This highlights that fairness 
for this group differs from fairness concerning groups with other protected attributes like 
age, gender, or race, primarily due to the vast diversity in how disabilities manifest and 
the sensitive nature of disability information, which is often not shared to avoid discrim-
ination [40].  
4. Exploring Future Directions 
As we explore the development of accessible interfaces for individuals with disabili-
ties, there are several promising directions for futur e research. These directions aim to 
improve the accuracy, functionality, and user experience of sign language recognition sys-
tems by leveraging advanced AI technologies and ML. Below, we outline four key re-
search directions that could significantly enhance the field of accessibility and create more 
inclusive digital environments.  
4.1. Training a Sign Language Recognition Model with AI-Generated Images 
One potential area to explore is the use of AI -generated images to train SLR models. 
Training these models requires large datasets of sign language videos or images (since these 
models use supervised learning), which can be hard and time-consuming to obtain. By us-
ing images generated with artificial intelligence, scientists can create datasets that mimic  
real sign language movements. This approach has the potential to expand existing training 
data and allow models to learn a wider range of gestures and types of sign language.  
In addition, this method can solve the problems related to the lack of sign lan guage 
datasets for less commonly used sign languages. AI-generated data can provide a scalable 
solution for creating models that are more inclusive and adaptive. Future research should

focus on evaluating the effectiveness of AI -generated images to improve  model perfor-
mance and optimizing the token engineering used to generate these images to ensure they 
accurately reflect real gestures.  
4.2. Enhancing Model Output Using ChatGPT for Rephrasing 
Another interesting direction for future research is to integrate natural language pro-
cessing tools like ChatGPT to improve the results of sign language recognition models.  
Although these models can accurately identify and translate signs, the raw output can  
sometimes be unclear or sound strange in natural lang uage. By using large generative  
language models to reformulate the results, we could improve the fluency and readability 
of the recognized text, making the output more intuitive and conversational.  
This approach has the potential to improve the overall UX, especially for people who 
rely on this type of communication system. Users interact more seamlessly with this tech-
nology, making it easier for them to communicate more effectively. Further research could 
investigate the impact of this reformulation on user satisfaction and the overall quality of 
communication in different contexts, such as educational, professional , or social.

4.3. Sign Language to Enhanced Text (Using LLM) to Speech 
Another promising application refers to the conversion of the result of the sign lan-
guage recognition model into enhanced text using large generative language models, fol-
lowed by TTS. This development could have a transformative impact on people with  
speech and hearing impairments who need to deliver speeches/presentations or partici-
pate in public speaking. By transl ating sign language into clear and structured spoken 
language, TTS systems can help users effectively communicate their messages to a wider 
audience without relying on intermediaries such as translators.  
In addition, integrating tools such as ChatGPT to rephrase the result can ensure that 
the generated speech is not only accurate but also designed to sound natural and  engag-
ing. This would allow users to retain full control over their communication and support  
greater independence in professional, educational, and social settings.

4.4. Translating Sign Language Model Output into Multiple Languages 
In today’s globalized world, language barriers present significant challenges for acces-
sibility. To address this problem, we extend the use of ChatGPT to translate the output of 
sign language recognition models based on the available languages. This allows the system 
to not only recognize and annotate sign language to text but also provide translation when 
other languages are used. This feature improves the accessibility of online platforms for us-
ers who are hearing-impaired and who are non-native speakers of the primary language.

4.5. Real-Time Sign Language Interpretation in Livestreams 
An innovative direction for future research is the use of sign language recognition  
models for real-time interpretation during live streams. In this scenario, sign language can 
be interpreted on the fly and provide instant translation during live broadcasts, confer-
ences, or events. This significantly increases accessibility for people with hearing impair-
ments who participate in this type of real-time interaction.  
In this context, ChatGPT can be used to improve interpretation by rewriting the trans-
lated content in real-time, ensuring that the result is not only accurate but also contextu-
ally appropriate and clear to the audience. This can fill the communication gaps during 
conversations from events where the original output may be too literal or unclear. Future 
research could explore the technical challenges of implementing this system in real -time 
settings, focusing on latency, accuracy, and user feedback to ensure a smooth and effective 
experience for end users.  
5. Conclusions  
In order to write this systematic review, we conducted an extensive search across  
several key academic databases, including Google Scholar, Elsevier, and Springer. This  
initial search returned thousands of research papers that needed a thorough filtering pro-
cess. To refine these results and make sure they were closely aligned with the focus of our 
study, we used the PEDro and PRISMA methodologies. These frameworks allowed us  to 
systematically narrow down the pool of research to obtain more relevant and targeted  
studies that directly contribute to the objectives of our review.  
There are numerous studies that have been conducted with a focus on the development 
of accessibility solutions through the use of artificial intelligence and machine learning tech-
nologies, especially in the areas of speech recognition, sign language recognition , and 
speech-to-text and text-to-speech conversion. These research efforts highlight the growing 
interest in the field and the accessibility advancements for individuals with disabilities. 
The primary and most s ignificant findings from this study can be summarized as  
follows: 
• Speech-to-text and text-to-speech models have impressive accuracy (e.g., in the Ren, 
Y. article, they achieved a 99.84% accuracy of word-level intelligibility for an almost 
unsupervised algorithm of TTS machine learning [16]; the STT model built by Google 
demonstrated a similarity score of 97.116% after a robust evaluation [21]);  
• Whisper’s speech recognition algorithm outperformed other models with a word er-
ror rate of 2.9% [7]; the algorithm implemented by Darabkh, K. for speech recognition 
using a discrete wavelet transform with  a modified MFCC achieved a remarkable 
recognition rate of 98.86%;  
• Continuous sign language recognition needs improvement because the algorithms  
implemented by authors in the explored solutions did not achieve more than a 95%

Appl. Sci. 2024, 14, 10337 14 of 16 
 
accuracy (Pu, J. achieved a 93.9% accuracy [35], and Mittal, A. achieved a 72.3% ac-
curacy for signed sentences and a 89.5% accuracy for isolated words [3]); 
• Research interest in SLR models has increased significantly in the last two years; 
In conclusion, while technologies such as STT, TTS, and SR have achieved an impres-
sive level of maturity , this progress is largely attributed to the high accuracy rates  (less 
than 3.5% word error rates for Whisper and Speechmatics  [7]) of these systems and  their 
ability to offer outputs with good mean opinion scores (more than 4.38 mean opinion score 
for Tacotron 2 [15]), which serves as a key measure of user satisfaction. These  advance-
ments have made STT, TTS, and SR solutions highly effective and reliable for a wide range 
of users, including those with speech and hearing impairments. The accuracy of sign lan-
guage recognition systems remains a critical area where enhancements could lead  to bet-
ter usability and effectiveness for users. 
In order to address this challenge, exploring innovative approaches such as AI -gen-
erated images for training SLR models could result in insightful and promising results. 
This method could provide a more diverse dataset, helping, in this way, to capture differ-
ent variations in sign language. Furthermore, integrating advanced tools like generative 
large language models such as ChatGPT to rephrase and clarify the output from sign lan-
guage recognition systems could be a game-changer. Such integration would not only en-
hance the clarity of communication but also ensure that the expressions used by humans 
in sign language are accurately transposed to those who may not be fluent in it.  
A particular challenge lies in enhanci ng the performance of sign language recogni-
tion systems when applied to continuous video streams. Unlike image-based recognition, 
where individual signs can be accurately identified, video -based recognition requires the 
system to handle dynamic and fluid s equences of signs, often leading to decreased accu-
racy. Addressing this challenge is crucial for developing more effective and reliable sign 
language recognition technologies, especially for real -time applications such as live com-
munication and education. Overcoming these obstacles will be an important step toward 
offering an accessible experience for users who rely on sign language. 
These advancements could play a pivotal role in bridging the communication gap for 
sign language users, ultimately  contributing to a more inclusive and accessible environ-
ment. As the field continues to evolve, focusing on these innovative strategies will be es-
sential for enabling better interaction and understanding among diverse communities.
