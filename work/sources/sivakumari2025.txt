Abstract. Sign language bridges communication gaps, enabling hearing 
impaired people to understand and connect among themselves. Sign language 
recognition (SLR) is an automated algorithm that translates hand gestures from 
hearing impaired into text for facilitating interactions with normal people. In 
recent years with the explosion of research in technologies like computer vision, 
deep learning and natural language processing, significant advancements have 
been made in SLR, improving its accuracy and real-world applicability. 
Specifically, Continuous Sign Language Recognition (CSLR) is a challenging 
task as it aims to translate video captured gestures into meaningful text 
sequentially. Unlike word level sign recognition, CSLR should handle complex 
spatial-temporal dependencies, coarticulation effects, and variations in signer 
style. This paper provides a critical review of recent advances in CSLR, focusing 
on deep learning-based approaches, dataset challenges, feature extraction 
techniques, and evaluation metrics. We conclude the review by providing 
potential future research directions based on the limitations in the current 
methodologies. 
Keywords:Sign language recognition, deep learning, computer vision, natural 
language processing, SLR datasets.

neural networks (CNNs) like GoogleNet [11] and ResNet [12] popular for spatial 
feature extraction. Temporal modelling is achieved using networks such as recurrent 
neural networks (RNNs) [13] and temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) [14]. 
Some models, like C3D and I3D, use 3D CNNs to extract spatio-temporal features 
simultaneously. More recently, Transformer models and their variants have gained 
attention for sign language recognition, achieving improved performance. Metrics like 
accuracy, recall, precision and F1 score are used for evaluating the WLSLR models.

3 CSLR – Deep Learning based Recognition Methods 
Deep learning-based recognition methods for WLSLR and CSLR captures a larger 
feature space, leading to better recognition accuracy when compared traditional ma-
chine learning based models. This section reviews the methods for CSLR using spa-
tial, temporal and hybrid features. In addition to CNNs, RNNs, transformers, we pre-
sent the latest trend of using multi modal fusion to leverage the characteristics of both 
visual and text features.  
3.1 Methods based on CNN and RNNs 
CNNs have proved to an excellent feature extractio n tool on images which is lever-
aged to frames of videos in CSLR [16]. They capture good spatial information that is 
ingrained in the video frames. To extract temporal information that defines the chang-
es in the hand and body movements of the signer, recurrent phenomena is engaged on 
the spatial features. This builds a combination of both CNN – RNN model for CSLR 
[17][18]. The combination has shown to extract spatio – temporal visual features rep-
resenting the sign videos. Models like Resnet – 18 [19] and Resnet – 50 [20] were 
used as baseline models in CSLR. Similarly, RNN models that follow the CNN are 
long short-term memory (LSTM) [21] and gated recurrent units (GRU) [22]. Table 2 
shows the latest CNN – RNN models used in building CSLR. Subsequently, pre-
trained models like GoogleNet [11] and hybrid approaches, such as CNN-LSTM-
HMM [23], improved performance but increase complexity. Studies explore different 
CNN-HMM structures for computational efficiency. Some approaches use motion

.2 Methods based on Transformers 
In recent years, Transformer models and their variants have gained immense populari-
ty in feature extraction for CSLR an d continuous sign language translation (CSLT) 
[28]. Originally developed for natural language processing (NLP) [29], transformers 
excel at capturing global dependencies within large datasets. Given sign language's 
unique grammatical structure, researchers have extended Transformer-based architec-
tures to CSLR and CSLT tasks, often integrating both into a single framework. CSLR 
generates gloss sequences that follow the same order as sign language gestures

whereas CSLT converts gloss sequences into spoken language text, requiring a reor-
dering of the sequence to align with natural language syntax. 
In Multi-Branch Network (Shin et al.) [30], a combination of convolutional layers 
and transformer layers were employed to extract both local and long-range dependen-
cies in sign language sequences. Video Transformer Network (VTN) (De Coster et 
al.) [31] trained a vision transformer (ViT) using features extracted from ResNet-34 
(pretrained on ImageNet) and pose data obtained from OpenPose BODY135. Evalu-
ated on AUTSL isolated sign recognition dataset, achieving 92.92% accuracy. The 
study uniformly down sampled video frames, which could lead to loss of important 
sign features, as sign language gestures are not evenly distributed in time. Another 
noticeable model was Mask-Future Transformer (Du et al.) [32], which applied swin-
transformer for semantic features and proposes a Mask-Future Transformer for tem-
poral modelling by computing self-attention without future frames to simulate real-
time comprehension. Despite better performance, the two transformers made training 
difficult and computationally expensive. Transformer models have revolutionized 
sign language recognition and translation by efficiently capturing spatial and temporal 
dependencies. Hybrid models combining CNNs with Transformers offer state- of-the-
art performance, but challenges like computational cost and data processing still need 
to be addressed. Future research should focus on efficient Transformer architectures 
and better real-time applications for sign language understanding. 
3.3 Methods based on Multi Modals 
Spatio-temporal visual features are crucial for Continuous Sign Language Recogni-
tion (CSLR) as they capture both spatial and temporal context in sign language vide-
os. Recent advancements in feature extraction models have focused on improving 
context representation across frames to enhance recognition accuracy. This summary 
explores key methodologies, challenges, and improvements in spatial-temporal mod-
elling, motion feature extraction, and cross-modal learning for CSLR. Temporal Dif-
ference Networks (TDN) [33] for Visual Features was applied to CSLR video data to 
learn the difference between consecutive frames and segments. The computed differ-
ences between frames introduce deviation in weight computation, affecting the overall 
quality of extracted visual features. Temporal Semantic Pyramid (TSM) [34] network 
addresses the semantic inconsistencies between frames using inter-scale and intra-
scale attention mechanisms with increased computational costs. 
To overcome the above shortcoming, multi modal methods were proposed to align 
gloss features with spatial feature extraction by computing contrastive cross-modal 
loss. Contrastive cross-modal loss [35][36] balances redundant information between 
visual features and BERT-extracted gloss features. This loss optimizes the extracted 
visual features, making them more robust for CSLR [37]. Inspiration from Multi-
Modal Learning in Vision and Language was used in CSLR to construct graph em-
beddings for improving representation learning [38]. Recent works in image caption-
ing and image-text matching were used to optimize multi-modal loss between visual 
and textual embeddings in CSLR [39][40]. Pre-trained language models like BERT 
and T5 have Transformer-based architectures that efficiently capture contextual de-

4 Recognition in CSLR 
The isolated SLR is treated as a classification task, typically solved using SoftMax or 
SVM. In contrast, CSLR involves recognizing a sequence of signs, making it a se-
quence-to-sequence (seq2seq) problem. Two primary approaches adapted from natu-
ral language processing (NLP) for continuous SLR are Connectionist Temporal Clas-
sification (CTC) and the Encoder-Decoder Network. These methods help effectively 
model and recognize sequential sign language gestures. 
4.1 Connectionist temporal Classification

(2006) [43] as a solution for sequence- to-sequence (seq2seq) problems without re-
quiring fine-grained labels. It became widely used in weakly supervised learning, 
particularly for tasks where input and output sequences have different lengths, such as 
speech recognition and continuous sign language recognition (CSLR).

4.2 Encoder – Decoder Network 
The Encoder-Decoder Network is widely used in CSLR to align input video sequenc-
es with target language sequences [55]. The encoder encodes the input into a fixed-
size latent vector, while the decoder aligns and generates the output sequence. Early 
approaches relied on RNN-based architectures [56], but they suffered from long-term 
dependency issues, which led to the adoption of attention mechanisms [57] and Trans-
former-based models [58]. Transformers, being an Encoder-Decoder structure, use 
multi-head attention to globally link features, achieving strong performance in CSLR. 
Despite their effectiveness, Encoder-Decoder networks require high computational 
power and can be difficult to train. Techniques like pre-training, key pooling, and 
latent space optimization help improve convergence. Additionally, most encoder-
decoder networks lack bi-directional decoding, which may cause information loss.

While BERT can address this, it comes at a high computational cost, making it im-

5 Conclusions 
Continuous Sign language recognition (CSLR) is a essential research area with signif-
icant social impact, as it facilitates communication between the hearing impaired and 
normal people. Automated SLR plays a key role in bridging this gap, making ad-
vancements in this field highly relevant. This review provides an overview of sign 
language recognition, covering traditional methods and focusing more elaborately 
deep learning-based approaches for CSLR. It examines key aspects such as datasets, 
evaluation metrics, methods, challenges, and future research directions. We highlight 
the latest state- of-the-art methods, particularly transformer-based and multi modal 
methods. Additionally, we compare the advantages and limitations of various recogni-
tion techniques. We conclude that the increasing availability of large datasets will 
enable more reliable validation of deep learning models, with significant challenges 
such as signer independence, model complexity, and real-world applicability.  The in-
depth analysis and proposed potential research directions described in this review can 
hopefully help in addressing these challenges.
