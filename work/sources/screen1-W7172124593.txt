 Abstract

More than a million deaf and hard-of-hearing people worldwide communicate through sign language. However, there is a substantial communication barrier between the deaf and hearing communities, as most hearing-abled people lack proficiency in sign language. Sign Language Recognition (SLR) systems have therefore emerged as a vital technological solution to overcome this communication divide by automatically translating sign language into spoken or written forms. This paper reviews the evolution of SLR systems, from early sensor and handcrafted-feature-based approaches to modern vision and deep-learning-based methods. We examine the progressive improvement in SLR from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), transformer models, and multimodal architectures from isolated word-level to continuous sentence-level recognition. We also discuss the emergence of recent multimodal, large-scale datasets that promise to support robust training and evaluation of flexible, generalized SLR systems. Finally, we highlight key challenges (e.g., data scarcity, signer variability, integration of non-manual cues, and real-time performance), thereby proposing future research directions to develop universal, all-inclusive sign language recognition systems that bridge communication gaps between the deaf and hearing worlds.
